About Granada for Foreign Buyers

Granada, on the shore of Lake Nicaragua, is one of Central America's oldest colonial cities, and its restored courtyard houses are what draw most foreign buyers. Foreigners can buy property here in their own name: The US State Department reports no specific restrictions on foreign or non-resident investors apart from border and national-security areas, and a standard purchase needs no residency permit, local partner or minimum investment. The 2025 constitution protects property in Article 40 but allows expropriation 'with fair compensation', and the same State Department report documents seizures of foreign-owned property. In August 2026 the government took nine homes owned by US retirees in Tola, Rivas; eight were returned in October.

The real work is the title. Purchases are signed as an escritura pública before a Nicaraguan notary and then entered in the Registro Público under Law 698 of 2009. The US Embassy estimates about 28,000 properties were confiscated between 1979 and 1990 and many remain disputed, and colonial houses tend to have the longest ownership chains, so a full title study by your own lawyer is essential before any deposit.

Buyers should also weigh the political backdrop. As of May 2026 the US State Department rates Nicaragua Level 3, 'Reconsider travel', citing crime, limited healthcare, wrongful detention and other risks. Granada's prices are low by regional standards, but liquidity is thinner than in Costa Rica or Panama, so plan for a longer hold and a slower resale.

Granada Real Estate FAQ

Can foreigners buy property in Granada, Nicaragua?

Legally, yes. The US State Department reports no specific restrictions on foreign or non-resident buyers apart from border and national-security areas, including a 15 km strip along land borders declared state property in 2025. But the same report says the government has seized foreign-owned property, so treat any purchase as high-risk.

What is the biggest risk when buying in Granada?

Title history. The US Embassy estimates around 28,000 properties were confiscated between 1979 and 1990 and many are still disputed. Hire your own lawyer, order a full title study at the Registro Público, and never rely on an unregistered deed.

Is it safe to buy property in Nicaragua right now?

The US State Department rated Nicaragua Level 3, 'Reconsider travel', in its May 2026 advisory, citing crime, limited healthcare and the risk of wrongful detention. In August 2026 the government also took nine homes owned by US retirees in Tola, Rivas, so it is a much higher-risk market than Costa Rica or Panama.

Can I get residency in Nicaragua as a retiree?

Possibly, but the rules changed: the old pensionado law (Law 694) was repealed by the 2024 General Tourism Law (Law 1210), and retiree residency now falls under the general migration law. Confirm the current requirements and income threshold with the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería or an immigration lawyer before applying.
