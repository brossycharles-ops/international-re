Latin America Real Estate News This Week: October 12–17, 2026

By Charles Brossy October 17, 2026 7 min read

This week's Latin America real estate news for foreign buyers: Nicaragua seized and then began returning beach homes owned by US retirees, Uruguay raised interest rates, Peru held rates despite rising inflation, Ecuador announced a new curfew across its coast, and Brazil's real jumped ahead of an October 25 presidential runoff. Buenos Aires asking prices barely moved. Here is what happened between October 5 and 17, 2026, and what each item means if you are buying property in the region.

Nicaragua: Retirees' Beach Homes Seized, Then Partly Returned

This is the week's most important story for foreign owners. On October 12 the independent outlet Confidencial reported that Nicaragua's Procuraduría General de Justicia (PGJ, the attorney general's office) had taken nine luxury homes owned by US retirees on Playa Gavilán, in Tola (Rivas), in late August and donated them to the INSS, the social security institute. The homes reportedly cost roughly US$500,000 to US$800,000 each to build, and one owner put the group's losses at about US$15 million. According to the report, an INSS official then offered to rent the houses back to their owners for about US$2,000 a month. One owner told Confidencial: “It’s a mockery: they take our houses and then they want to rent them back to us.”

The ownership structure explains how this happened. Under Ley 690, the coastal-zone development law, the owners held title to the buildings, but the land belonged to the State, and they paid the Tola municipality US$1,000 a year. Infobae reported on October 16 that the INSS began returning homes on October 15, three days after the story broke. There has been no official explanation, and it is unclear whether all nine will be returned.

What it means: on the Nicaraguan coast, check whether you are buying full title to the land or only the building on state land. Then price in the political risk. Our San Juan del Sur guide and the Granada city guide cover title checks, and the US State Department currently rates Nicaragua Level 3, “Reconsider travel.”

Brazil: A Runoff on October 25 and a Stronger Real

In the October 4 first round, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro finished ahead of President Lula, by about 47% to 45%, so the two face a runoff on October 25. Markets cheered: on Monday, October 5 the dollar fell 4.12% against the real to close at about R$5.00, and the Ibovespa rose 7.70% to a record 206,911.89. Polls published October 14–15 by Quaest and Datafolha both showed Flávio at 51% and Lula at 49% of valid votes, a gap within the margin of error.

What it means: a 4% move in a day makes property priced in reais more expensive for dollar buyers. The runoff could move the currency just as sharply in either direction. If you are close to signing in São Paulo or Florianópolis, consider timing your currency conversion around October 25. Our Brazil buying guide covers the rules.

Uruguay: Policy Rate Raised to 6%

On Thursday, October 8, the Banco Central del Uruguay's monetary policy committee voted unanimously to raise its policy rate by 25 basis points, from 5.75% to 6.00%. Annual inflation reached 4.7% in September, above the 4.5% target, and average inflation expectations stood at 4.71%.

What it means: borrowing in pesos gets dearer. Most Uruguayan property is priced in US dollars, so asking prices for foreign buyers are not directly affected. Higher rates tend to support the peso, however, which raises the dollar cost of peso-priced running costs. See our Uruguay guide for foreign buyers.

Peru: A Surprise Hold at 4.25%

On Wednesday, October 7, the Banco Central de Reserva del Perú kept its reference rate at 4.25%, a 13th consecutive monthly hold by EFE's count. Nine of the 13 analysts in a Bloomberg survey had expected a hike. Annual inflation rose from 4.4% in August to 4.5% in September, above the 1–3% target range. The next decision is on November 12.

What it means: if inflation keeps rising, sol mortgage rates could rise too. Cash buyers are unaffected, but this is a good moment to understand Peru's 3% buyer transfer tax. Our new tip explains how Peru's alcabala tax works.

Ecuador: A New Curfew on the Coast from November 1

President Daniel Noboa signed Executive Decree 519 in Manta on October 15. It imposes a curfew from midnight to 5:00 a.m. for ten days from November 1 in five coastal provinces: Guayas, Manabí, Los Ríos, El Oro and Esmeraldas. Santa Elena is not included. The decree amends the state of exception declared in September for “grave internal unrest.” Pichincha and Santo Domingo de los Tsáchilas remain under the state of exception, but without a curfew.

What it means: Manabí includes Manta and Puerto López, popular with foreign buyers, and Guayas includes Guayaquil. Plan viewings and closings around the curfew, and treat security as a central part of your due diligence on the coast. Inland Cuenca, in Azuay, is outside the curfew zone; see our Cuenca neighbourhood guide.

Argentina: Buenos Aires Prices Edge Up 0.3%

Zonaprop's September report put the average asking price for apartments in the city of Buenos Aires at US$2,484 per square meter, up 0.3% on the month and 1.4% so far in 2026. Prices remain 11.3% below the series peak. The gross rental yield held at 5.70% a year, while rents were up 28.7% year on year in pesos. Villa Riachuelo posted the largest 12-month rise of any neighbourhood, at 11.14%.

What it means: the price recovery has slowed to a crawl, which leaves room to negotiate. Our October 2026 market update has the longer-term trend.

Costa Rica and Mexico: Rate Cuts Back on the Table

In Costa Rica, minutes of the September 24 meeting, reported by La Nación on October 13, showed the Banco Central de Costa Rica's board voted 5–2 to keep the policy rate at 3.00%. The bank's own economics staff had recommended a 25-basis-point cut, and the two dissenters wanted one. Under the 2026 calendar there is no October meeting; the next decision is on November 26.

In Mexico, minutes of Banxico's September 24 decision, published October 8, showed a board divided on further cuts after a unanimous hold at 6.50%. Some members said a cut could be considered if conditions allow.

What it means: local-currency mortgages may get cheaper in both countries. For dollar buyers, lower rates can weaken the local currency, which lowers the dollar cost of construction and local fees.
