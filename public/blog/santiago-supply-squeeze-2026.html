Santiago, Chile Real Estate Investment in 2026: What the Supply Squeeze Means

By Charles Brossy April 17, 2026 · Updated October 23, 2026 9 min read

Is Santiago, Chile real estate investment worth a serious look in 2026? The supply side is the strongest part of the case. Chile authorised just 69,058 new homes in 2025, 19.7% fewer than in 2024 and 47.7% below the 2000–2024 average, according to the Cámara Chilena de la Construcción (CChC) using INE data; in the Santiago Metropolitan Region, the floor area approved in building permits fell 23%. But a thin pipeline is not the same thing as a shortage today. Greater Santiago still has more than two years of unsold new apartments on the market, the economy is slowing, and the peso has swung sharply this year. Here is what the data actually shows, updated for October 2026.

The Numbers Behind the Squeeze

Start with inventory, because it tempers the shortage story. At the end of July 2026 there were 49,834 new apartments for sale in the Metropolitan Region, down 18.2% from 60,893 a year earlier, according to TOCTOC’s InfoInmobiliario report; 46.8% of them were ready to move into. Net sales of new apartments in Greater Santiago from January to July rose 6.7% year on year to 12,110 units. At that pace the apartment stock would take about 28.3 months to clear (22.9 months for houses). The stock is shrinking, then, but it has not run out.

Prices are firming. In the same TOCTOC report, new apartments averaged 83 UF per square metre in the rolling quarter to July 2026, 5.4% more than a year earlier. The eastern comunas where foreign buyers usually look are well above that (non-subsidised segment):

  • Vitacura: 117 UF/m² (about US$4,960/m²). The most expensive comuna in the report.
  • Las Condes: 114 UF/m² (about US$4,830/m²).
  • Providencia: 103 UF/m² (about US$4,360/m²). On the future Línea 7 route.

The dollar figures are our own conversion, using the SII’s UF value of CLP 41,205.16 for 23 October 2026 and the observed dollar of CLP 972.60 on 1 October 2026. They are asking prices for new units, not resale transaction prices, so treat them as a ceiling for comparable used stock.

Nationally, the BIS residential property price series for Chile rose 5.1% in nominal terms in the year to Q1 2026, or 2.4% after inflation. That is steady, not explosive, and we have found no named forecast for the rest of 2026 that we would ask you to rely on.

Rental Yields: Not Spectacular, But Reliable

Chilean yields are not Guanacaste. You’re not running vacation-rental numbers here. What you get in Santiago is something different: consistency, with caveats.

Global Property Guide’s Q1 2026 survey puts Santiago’s average gross apartment yield at 4.91%, with apartments ranging from 3.84% to 5.85%, and the national average at 4.82%. These are gross figures: property tax, common expenses (gastos comunes), vacancy, management and income tax all come off before you see a net return.

Occupancy is high. TOCTOC’s InfoRenta report for May 2026 put occupancy of the Metropolitan Region’s 477,900 rental apartments at 97.9%. In the eastern zone, vacancy was close to 1% and asking rents rose about 12% in UF over the year, to 0.37 UF/m² a month. The picture is not uniform, though. In the professionally managed multifamily segment, CBRE counted 50,933 operating units in the first half of 2026 and another 7,429 under construction. Occupancy in operating buildings was 94.6%, but the weighted average rent of 0.271 UF/m² a month was 3.2% lower than a year earlier, and rents in the Centro Norte zone fell 11%. Rents are rising in the east and falling in parts of the centre, so averages will not tell you what a specific building will earn.

The Metro Catalyst

Santiago is building two new Metro lines, but on very different timelines:

  • Línea 7: 26 km and 19 stations across eight comunas: Renca, Cerro Navia, Quinta Normal, Santiago, Recoleta, Providencia, Las Condes and Vitacura, with a Renca–Vitacura trip of 37 minutes. Metro reported 40% overall progress at the end of January 2026 (the latest figure we found) and expects the line to open at the end of 2028.
  • Línea 9: about 27 km and 19 stations running south along Avenida Santa Rosa through Recoleta, Santiago, San Miguel, San Joaquín, La Granja, San Ramón, La Pintana and Puente Alto. It opens in stages: the first stretch, from Bío Bío to Plaza de La Pintana, is planned for 2030, the northern stretch to Puente Cal y Canto for 2032 and the southern stretch to Plaza de Puente Alto for 2033.

New lines can lift values near stations, but we have not found a Santiago-specific study with a number we would ask you to bank on, and some of any premium tends to be priced in once routes are public. Treat Línea 7 as a long-dated amenity that improves resale appeal in its corridor, not as a guaranteed uplift. Línea 9 matters more for rental catchment in the south of the city than for the eastern comunas most foreign buyers target.

The Macro Picture: Tailwinds and Headwinds

The Banco Central de Chile cut its policy rate to 4.5% in December 2025 and held it there at every meeting of 2026 through 8 September, unanimously each time. That is far below the 11.25% peak, set in October 2022 and held until the first cut in July 2023. Lower rates help Chilean buyers, who need bank finance, and so support demand for the stock that foreign cash buyers also target. There is also policy support for new homes: a temporary mortgage-rate subsidy was recently expanded to 80,000 places for homes worth up to 6,000 UF, running until 31 May 2028.

The headwinds are just as real. In its September 2026 Monetary Policy Report the central bank cut its 2026 GDP growth forecast to 0.25–0.75% (from 1–1.75% in June), pointed to continued weakness in the real-estate sector, and noted inflation hovering around 4%, which it expects to return to about 3% in the second quarter of 2027.

Currency is the risk dollar investors underestimate most. The peso has weakened this year: the SII-published observed dollar went from its 2026 low of CLP 854.25 on 10 February to CLP 972.60 on 1 October 2026, about 14% more pesos per dollar. That makes peso-priced property cheaper for a dollar buyer today. The same move in reverse hurts you later: rents and resale prices are set in UF or pesos, so a further fall in the peso cuts your dollar income and your exit value. We know of no exchange-rate forecast reliable enough to plan a purchase around.

Copper is a big driver. Chile mined an estimated 5.3 million tonnes of copper in 2025, about 23% of world output of 23 million tonnes, according to the USGS, and the central bank raised its medium-term copper price assumption to US$5.60 per pound in the September report. A copper downturn would hit Chile’s terms of trade and, typically, the peso.

The institutional backdrop remains solid. S&P affirmed Chile at A (A+ in local currency) with a stable outlook on 25 September 2026, and Fitch affirmed A− with a stable outlook on 21 September 2026. Chile runs a floating exchange rate, and its foreign-investment law (Law 20.848) gives covered investors (those bringing US$5 million or more, so not a typical apartment buyer) access to the formal exchange market to send invested capital and net profits abroad once taxes are paid; capital brought in from abroad also falls under the Central Bank’s Chapter XIV foreign-exchange rules, so route funds through a Chilean bank and keep the paperwork. These aren’t glamorous advantages, but they matter when you’re managing an asset from overseas.

The Foreign Buyer Advantage

Chile is one of the more straightforward countries in Latin America for foreign ownership. Article 57 of the Civil Code says the law draws no distinction between Chileans and foreigners in acquiring and enjoying civil rights, and buying an ordinary Santiago apartment needs no special foreign-buyer approval. The main exception, Decree-Law 1939, restricts nationals of neighbouring countries from owning property in declared border zones, which does not affect central Santiago. For a comuna-by-comuna breakdown, see our Santiago neighborhood guide for property buyers.

You’ll need a RUT (tax ID) from the SII, which lists buying real estate among the investments for which non-residents must obtain one; non-residents earning Chilean income must also name a representative in Chile. Budget separately for the notary, registration at the Conservador de Bienes Raíces, legal due diligence and any agent’s commission. Published estimates of the total vary, so get a written quote from your lawyer. If you buy new from a developer, note that sales by habitual sellers have been subject to IVA (VAT) since 2016. The Reconstruction Law promulgated on 9 October 2026 provides a one-year IVA exemption on the first sale of completed new homes, with no value or size cap, expected to start on 1 December 2026; which units qualify depends on the final rules, and off-plan units may not. Ask your lawyer whether it changes the price of a unit you are negotiating now.

Financing is the catch. Getting a Chilean mortgage as a foreigner is difficult, and most banks decline applicants with temporary residency or none, so plan on paying cash. Being a cash buyer is not a protection, though. When you sell, your buyer will most likely be a Chilean who needs a mortgage at whatever rates apply then, and with more than two years of new stock on the market you will be competing with developers for that buyer.

Where to Focus

Given the data, three approaches stand out for 2026:

  1. Small apartments in Providencia and Las Condes. One-bedroom units yield more than the city average in Global Property Guide’s data, and eastern-zone vacancy is near 1%. But new-unit prices of 103–114 UF/m² squeeze yields, so check the actual rent for that building before you buy. Both comunas are on the Línea 7 route.
  2. Selective buys in the Línea 7 corridor. Buy only if the deal works on today’s rent, with Línea 7 as upside after its planned 2028 opening. Check how close the unit is to station works, which can mean years of disruption.
  3. Vitacura for capital preservation. Lower yields and the city’s highest new-unit prices (117 UF/m²). It suits buyers who care more about holding value in a premium location than about income.

The Bottom Line

Santiago is not a yield story. It’s a stability story with moderate appreciation. The record-low permit numbers should mean fewer new completions in the years ahead, rates are well below their peak, and occupancy is high, especially in the east. Against that, there are still about 28 months of new apartments to sell, the central bank expects growth below 1% this year, and your dollar returns depend on a volatile peso.

For investors who want Latin American real estate exposure inside a strong institutional framework, Chile remains one of the region’s most dependable entry points. Buy a specific building at a rent you have verified, in cash you can afford to leave in pesos for years. Don’t buy on the strength of a squeeze that the inventory numbers say has not fully arrived.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners buy property in Santiago, Chile?

Yes. Article 57 of Chile’s Civil Code draws no distinction between Chileans and foreigners in acquiring and enjoying civil rights. You need a RUT (tax ID) from the SII, which lists buying real estate as an investment that requires one. The main restriction, Decree-Law 1939, applies to nationals of neighbouring countries in declared border zones, not to apartments in Santiago.

What rental yield can I expect in Santiago in 2026?

Global Property Guide puts Santiago’s average gross apartment yield at 4.91% in Q1 2026 (4.82% nationally), with apartments ranging from 3.84% to 5.85%. Net yields are lower after property tax, common expenses, vacancy, management and income tax. Occupancy of Metropolitan Region rental apartments was 97.9% in May 2026, according to TOCTOC.

Can non-residents get a mortgage in Chile?

Rarely. Getting a Chilean mortgage as a foreigner is difficult, and most banks decline applicants with temporary residency or none, so most foreign buyers should plan to pay cash. Remember that prices and rents are in UF or pesos: the observed dollar moved from CLP 854.25 in February 2026 to CLP 972.60 on 1 October 2026, and moves like that affect your returns in dollars.
