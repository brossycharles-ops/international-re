Buying Property in Granada, Nicaragua in 2026: What Foreigners Need to Know About the Risks

By Charles Brossy April 8, 2026 · Updated October 19, 2026 8 min read

Can a foreigner still buy property in Granada, Nicaragua in 2026? Legally, yes: there is no general ban on foreign or non-resident buyers. But the risk has risen sharply. The US government reports that Nicaragua's government has seized private and foreign-owned property, rewrote the constitution in 2025, and in August 2026 took nine homes owned by US retirees on the Pacific coast. Tourist arrivals are also well below their 2017 peak. Granada is cheap for a reason. Here is what the official data shows, sourced at the end.

Granada at a Glance

Granada sits on the north-western shore of Lake Nicaragua. Founded in 1524, it is one of the oldest Spanish colonial cities on the American mainland still on its original site, according to its UNESCO Tentative List entry. The municipality had about 135,000 residents in INIDE's 2023 estimate. Its draws for foreign buyers are restored courtyard houses in the colonial centre, the lake and the Isletas de Granada (Britannica counts about 300 islands within 8 km of the city), and a low cost of living: Numbeo estimates a single person's monthly costs in Managua at about $680 excluding rent, though a family of four needs about $2,400 before rent.

What Property Costs: Little Reliable Data

We found no official house-price index for Nicaragua, and Global Property Guide no longer publishes a Nicaragua page. Per-square-metre prices quoted online for Granada mostly come from agency blogs and listings, and we could not verify them, so we don't repeat them here. The best crowd-sourced indicator, Numbeo's Managua data (October 2026), puts city-centre apartments at about $1,190/m² and those outside the centre at about $680/m². For comparison, Zonaprop puts Palermo in Buenos Aires at about $3,450/m² (September 2026) and TOCTOC puts new apartments in Santiago's Providencia at about $4,360/m².

The same goes for rental yields: we found no reliable published data for Granada, so treat any "8–11% yield" claim with suspicion. Ask for the actual rental history of the property, and remember that tourist demand has been falling (below).

Foreign-visitor spending was US$527 million in 2025, up only 3.2% on 2024. Air arrivals fell about 40% between 2023 and 2025, according to Infobae, though 2023 was inflated by migrants transiting Managua. The tourism institute INTUR has announced a 2026 plan with seven strategic pillars, including the design of 16 new tourism products, and visitors' average daily spend rose to about $52 in the third quarter of 2025. But the overall visitor base is still about 40% below 2017.

Ownership Rules After the 2025 Constitution

The US State Department's 2026 Investment Climate Statement says there are "no specific restrictions regarding foreign or non-resident investors aside from certain border and other properties considered important to national security". So a foreigner can still buy property in their own name; note that a company buying property needs a legal representative who is Nicaraguan or a legal resident. What has changed:

  • A new constitution. The 2025 reform (Ley 1234) renumbered the constitution. Property is now protected in Article 40, which allows expropriation "with fair compensation" and prohibits confiscation. The old clause making officials personally liable for confiscations is gone.
  • A 15 km border strip. Ley 1258 (August 2025) declared a 15 km strip along all land borders state property and put it under military oversight. The US government called it a "massive confiscation".
  • Shorelines. Under the coastal law (Ley 690), the first 50 metres inland from the sea's high-tide line are public, with a further 200-metre restricted zone. On natural lakes such as Lake Nicaragua the public strip is 5 metres.
  • Investment registration. The State Department reports that foreign investments must register with a new national investment commission headed by a presidential adviser who is under US sanctions. US buyers should screen sellers and counterparties against the OFAC sanctions list.

The Confiscation Risk

The State Department reports that the government has "reportedly weaponized its legal apparatus to orchestrate the theft of foreign-owned assets", stripped more than 5,600 NGOs of their legal status and assets, and moved the property registry under the Attorney General's office. In 2023 it cancelled the citizenship of 222 former political prisoners and 94 opponents and seized their property. Older title problems remain: the US Embassy estimates about 28,000 properties were expropriated between 1979 and 1990, and it advises "extreme caution", especially with coastal property.

The risk is current. In August 2026, according to La Prensa, the government took nine luxury homes owned by US retirees in Tola, Rivas, and transferred them to the social security institute (INSS); eight were returned in October. The US State Department rates Nicaragua Level 3, "Reconsider travel", citing crime, health, wrongful detention and arbitrary enforcement of local laws. Relations with Washington are worsening too: the US Trade Representative has set a tariff on Nicaraguan goods that do not qualify under CAFTA-DR, at 0% in 2026 and rising to 10% in 2027 and 15% in 2028.

On top come notary, registry and legal fees; ask your own lawyer for a written estimate before you sign. Prices are commonly quoted in US dollars, but Nicaragua is not dollarized. The córdoba has been fixed at 36.6243 per dollar since 1 January 2024, and since January 2025 domestic goods and services must be priced in córdobas.

Residency for Retirees Has Changed

The old pensionado law, Ley 694, was repealed by the 2024 General Tourism Law (Ley 1210). We could not confirm the current income threshold from an official source, so check with the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería or an immigration lawyer before planning a move around it.

If You Still Want to Buy

  1. Hire your own Nicaraguan lawyer, never the seller's, and order a full title search at the public registry, including the 1979–1990 chain of ownership.
  2. Screen the seller and any developer against the US OFAC sanctions list, and confirm your bank can send funds to Nicaragua.
  3. Avoid property near borders and shorelines, and anything once owned by opposition figures, NGOs or confiscated entities.
  4. Only commit money you could afford to lose, and plan for a long, uncertain resale.

The Bottom Line

Granada's colonial architecture and low living costs are real. But the claim that the legal framework protects foreign owners no longer holds: the US government reports seizures of foreign-owned property, the constitution was rewritten in 2025, and US retirees' homes were taken as recently as August 2026. Tourism, the main source of rental income, is about 40% below its 2017 peak, and there is no reliable price or yield data to anchor a valuation. For most foreign buyers looking for a similar lifestyle, Costa Rica or Panama will be the easier, more predictable choice. If you buy in Granada, treat it as a lifestyle purchase you can afford to lose, not an investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners buy property in Granada, Nicaragua?

Yes. The US State Department reports no specific restrictions on foreign or non-resident buyers apart from border and national-security areas, including a 15 km strip along land borders declared state property in 2025. But it also reports that the government has seized foreign-owned property.

Is it safe to buy property in Nicaragua in 2026?

It is high-risk. The US rates Nicaragua Level 3, "Reconsider travel", and its 2026 Investment Climate Statement reports seizures of foreign-owned assets. In August 2026 the government took nine homes owned by US retirees in Tola, Rivas; eight were returned in October.

What taxes do you pay when buying property in Nicaragua?

The main transfer tax is an income-tax withholding of 1% to 7% of the registered value, rising with price, withheld when the deed is registered; agree in writing who pays it. Annual property tax (IBI) is 1% of 80% of the cadastral value.
