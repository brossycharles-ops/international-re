Uruguay Real Estate for Foreigners 2026: The Complete Buying Guide

By Charles Brossy April 29, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026 12 min read

Can foreigners buy property in Uruguay? Yes. Uruguay’s investment law gives foreign investors the same treatment as nationals and requires no prior authorisation, and foreign individuals can buy homes, apartments and land anywhere in the country with a standard deed (escritura), with no trust, no residency requirement and no restricted coastal or border zone. The main limits apply to companies and foreign states buying rural land. What foreign buyers should plan for instead is cost: Montevideo’s best barrios ask about US$4,000 per m², and buyer costs run well above the 2% transfer tax once the notary and agent are paid.

Uruguay is a small country of about 3.5 million people (3,499,451 in the final 2023 census) with strong institutions by regional standards. The Economist Intelligence Unit’s 2025 Democracy Index rates it a full democracy and the highest-scoring country in Latin America; Transparency International’s 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index gives it 73 points, 17th of 182 countries and second in the Americas after Canada; and the World Justice Project’s 2025 Rule of Law Index ranks it 23rd of 143 and first in Latin America and the Caribbean. There are no limits on moving capital in or out and the exchange market is free.

Bottom line: foreigners buy on the same terms as Uruguayans, with direct deed ownership. Prices are high for the region (median asking prices of about US$2,200–4,200 per m² in central Montevideo barrios, about US$3,200–4,500 in Punta del Este), gross yields on asking prices are about 5–7% in Montevideo, and buyer costs, taxes and fees typically add several percent on top of the price. Residency is a separate process: buying property does not by itself give you residency.

The Legal Framework

Law 16.906 (1998) says foreign investors receive the same treatment as national investors and need no prior authorisation to invest. Multilaw’s 2026 guide to Uruguayan real estate puts it simply: there are no restrictions on foreigners owning or occupying real estate. In practice:

  • No trust structure: you hold title directly through an escritura signed before an escribano (a notary, who is also a lawyer).
  • No restricted zones for individuals: we found no coastal or border-zone restriction on foreign buyers, unlike Mexico’s restricted zone or Chile’s border-zone rule.
  • No residency or visa needed to buy: a foreign individual can buy as a non-resident. A foreign company that owns Uruguayan property must register with the tax office (DGI).
  • Free movement of capital: Uruguay XXI, the government investment agency, says there are no limits on transferring or repatriating income or capital and no prior permits are required.
  • Rural land: companies that own rural land or run farms must have their whole capital in nominative shares held by natural persons (Law 18.092, as amended), and companies controlled by foreign states or sovereign funds cannot be authorised to own rural land (Law 19.283). These rules do not affect an individual buying a home or apartment.

Title is recorded at the Registro de la Propiedad (Sección Inmobiliaria), run by the Dirección General de Registros. Your escribano studies the chain of title before you sign; do not skip this step even in a country with reliable registries.

The Step-by-Step Buying Process

  1. Find a property and an independent escribano Listings in Uruguay are normally priced in US dollars. Hire your own escribano rather than relying on the seller’s; the escribano drafts the contracts, studies the title, calculates and withholds the taxes, and registers the deed.
  2. Boleto de reserva (reservation agreement) A preliminary contract, drafted by the escribano, that takes the property off the market while the title is studied. According to InfoCasas, the deposit (seña) is usually about 10% of the price, with a penalty of a similar size if either side backs out. Purchases paid in instalments use a longer contract, the compromiso de compraventa.
  3. Title study The escribano checks the registry for mortgages, liens and other encumbrances, and the departmental and national tax accounts for arrears. InfoCasas puts this stage at about 30–45 days.
  4. Escritura (final deed) Both parties sign the deed before the escribano and the balance is paid, usually by bank transfer. The escribano withholds the transfer tax and any capital gains tax owed by the seller.
  5. Registration The escribano files the deed with the Registro de la Propiedad. Keep the registered copy: it is your proof of ownership.

We found no official figure for how long a typical purchase takes from offer to registered title, so we do not quote one; ask your escribano for a written timetable.

Punta del Este

Punta del Este is a summer market. According to El Observador (October 2025), Argentines are the largest group of buyers, followed by Uruguayans, with Brazilian interest growing. Short-term rental data from AirROI show average occupancy of about 38% over the 12 months to September 2026, and Airbtics puts the median listing’s annual revenue at about US$26,000 with 47% occupancy; the two sources give very different pictures. AirROI’s average gross bookings of about UYU 453,500 a year (about US$11,300) would be about 4% of a typical two-bedroom’s asking price of about US$279,000, while Airbtics’s median revenue would be about 9%, both before cleaning, management, fees and taxes. Underwrite on the lower figure. See our Punta del Este guide for the detail.

Colonia del Sacramento

Colonia’s historic quarter is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and the town is linked to Buenos Aires by ferry across the Río de la Plata. Apartments in the town ask about US$3,100–3,700 per m² on InfoCasas. We found no reliable rental-yield data for Colonia.

Residency Is Separate From Buying

Buying property does not by itself give you residency: we found no residence route for property buyers in the official sources. The main routes are:

RouteWhat the official sources say
Permanent residence (residencia legal permanente)Open to any foreigner who intends to live in Uruguay permanently. You show means of living sufficient to support yourself; Migración publishes no fixed income figure. Fee 557.30 UI (indexed units), plus 55.70 UI.
Retiree regime (Law 16.340)Optional regime for people who are retired: at least US$1,500 a month from a pension or other income from abroad, plus either a home worth at least US$100,000 that you keep for 10 years or US$100,000 in government bonds held at the state bank (BROU). It adds benefits such as duty-free import of household goods; it is not required to obtain residence.
Legal citizenshipConstitution article 75: after 3 years of habitual residence if you have a family in Uruguay, or 5 years without, plus good conduct and capital, property or a profession.

Uruguayan nationals can travel to the Schengen area without a visa (EU Regulation 2018/1806, Annex II). Note that naturalised foreigners receive ciudadanía legal, not nationality, and passports issued to legal citizens have had acceptance problems abroad: in 2025 Germany, France and Japan objected to a new passport format, and the government reverted it. Check the current position before counting on a Uruguayan passport. Our Uruguay residency guide covers the documents and fees.

The wealth tax is the one most foreign buyers miss. It applies to residents and non-residents alike above the threshold. Under the tax code (Título 14, art. 53), the annual rate cuts written into the scale stop at 1.5% for non-residents who pay no Uruguayan income tax (IRNR), so a non-resident who leaves the property empty pays the older scale, starting at 0.7% just above the threshold and rising to 1.5%. Because property is counted at the Catastro value, usually below market, the base is often smaller than the price. Our Uruguay property-tax guide explains each tax.

The new-resident tax holiday

Uruguay’s tax holiday is an income-tax rule, not a property rule. Under Law 20.446, people who become tax residents from January 1, 2026 can opt to pay the non-resident tax instead of IRPF on foreign dividends, interest and capital gains for the year they become resident plus the next ten, if they spend more than 183 days a year in Uruguay, invest more than UI 12.5 million in real estate (about US$2.07 million at the DGI’s October 28, 2026 UI value and its latest posted dollar rate, for September 30) or put at least UI 625,000 a year into qualifying funds, were not tax residents in the two previous years and have not used the earlier regime. See our guide to the tax holiday.

Risks to Weigh

  • Price: Pocitos, Punta Carretas and Punta del Este ask about US$3,700–4,500 per m², more than most cities in our 17-city comparison.
  • Seasonality: Punta del Este rentals depend on a short summer; AirROI’s 12-month occupancy average is about 38%.
  • Currency: prices are in dollars but taxes, building fees and many rents are in pesos, so the exchange rate moves your costs and net yield.
  • Asking prices are not sale prices: the indexes above are built from listings. Ask your agent and escribano for recent sale prices in the same building or street.

FAQ: Uruguay Real Estate for Foreigners

Can foreigners buy property in Uruguay?

Yes. Law 16.906 gives foreign investors the same treatment as Uruguayans, and foreign individuals can buy homes, apartments and land with a standard escritura, with no trust, no residency requirement and no restricted coastal zone. Companies owning rural land must have nominative shares held by individuals, and companies controlled by foreign states cannot be authorised to own rural land.

What are closing costs when buying in Uruguay?

Plan for about 5–9% of the price. The buyer pays 2% transfer tax (ITP) on the Catastro value, the escribano’s fee of 3% plus 22% VAT, and, if an agent is involved, a 3% commission plus VAT.

Does buying property in Uruguay give you residency?

No. Residency is a separate application. Permanent residence is open to any foreigner who shows means of living sufficient to support themselves, with no fixed income figure published by Migración. The optional regime of Law 16.340 for people who are retired asks for at least US$1,500 a month plus a home worth US$100,000 or US$100,000 in government bonds.

What taxes do foreign owners pay in Uruguay each year?

The departmental Contribución Inmobiliaria, the national Primaria tax of 0.15%–0.3% of the Catastro value, the wealth tax on net Uruguayan assets above about US$165,000 per person (rates from 0.1% or 0.7% up to 1.5%, depending on residence), and 12% on any rent.
