How to Buy Property in Costa Rica as a Foreigner: Complete 2026 Guide

By Charles Brossy May 6, 2026 15 min read
Published May 6, 2026 · Last updated May 6, 2026

In This Guide

Costa Rica is the most popular Latin American real estate market for foreign buyers, and it has been for over two decades. Stable democracy, no army, universal healthcare, strong environmental protections, and a cost of living that still undercuts the US and Canada by 30–50% in most categories. Add reliable internet, two international airports, and a time zone that overlaps with US business hours, and you have a country that attracts everyone from retirees and remote workers to developers and short-term rental investors.

But buying property abroad is not the same as browsing Zillow. Costa Rica has its own legal system, its own closing process, and a handful of traps that can cost you tens of thousands of dollars if you walk in without doing your homework. This guide covers everything: who can buy, what it costs, how the process works, what taxes you will owe, how to get residency, and which mistakes to avoid.

Everything here reflects the legal and market conditions as of Q1–Q2 2026. Where data may shift, we note it. Where you need a local attorney, we tell you.

Can Foreigners Buy Property in Costa Rica?

Yes. Foreigners have the same property ownership rights as Costa Rican citizens. This is guaranteed by the Costa Rican Constitution (Articles 19 and 45), which grants equal rights to nationals and foreigners on property ownership. You do not need a residency visa, a special permit, or a local partner. You can buy, hold, and sell titled property in your own name while on a tourist visa.

This makes Costa Rica one of the most open property markets in the world. Compare that to Mexico (restricted zones within 50 km of the coast require a bank trust), Thailand (foreigners cannot own land), or the Philippines (foreigners cannot own land outright). In Costa Rica, the playing field is level.

There is one major exception: the Maritime Zone (Zona Marítimo Terrestre, or ZMT). Under Law No. 6043, the first 200 meters inland from the high-tide line is divided into two strips:

  • Public Zone (first 50 meters): Cannot be owned or built on by anyone. This is public land, full stop.
  • Restricted Zone (next 150 meters): Cannot be titled as private property. It can only be leased through a concession granted by the local municipality. Foreigners can hold a concession only through a Costa Rican corporation where at least 50% of shares are held by Costa Rican citizens, or through a trust (fideicomiso).

Outside the Maritime Zone, there are no restrictions whatsoever. If the property has a registered title (escritura) in the Registro Nacional, a foreigner can buy it outright.

How Much Does Property Cost in Costa Rica?

Costa Rica is not the cheapest market in Latin America — that distinction goes to countries like Nicaragua, Colombia, or Argentina — but it offers a combination of infrastructure, legal security, and lifestyle quality that justifies higher prices. Here is what to expect by area as of Q1 2026. Note: these are asking prices. Final sale prices often come in 5–15% below asking, depending on how motivated the seller is.

Guanacaste Coast (Tamarindo, Flamingo, Papagayo)

  • Condos: $200,000–$600,000
  • Single-family homes/villas: $500,000–$2,000,000+
  • Building lots: $80,000–$300,000 depending on ocean view and proximity to town

Tamarindo is the most liquid market on the Pacific coast. High short-term rental demand, good airport access (Liberia, LIR), and a well-established expat community.

Central Pacific (Manuel Antonio, Dominical, Uvita)

  • Condos and homes: $250,000–$800,000
  • Ocean-view lots: $100,000–$400,000

Manuel Antonio commands premium prices due to the national park and strong tourism traffic. Uvita and Dominical are growing fast but still offer relative value compared to Guanacaste.

Nosara and Samara

  • Homes: $300,000–$700,000
  • Lots: $100,000–$250,000

Nosara has become a wellness and surf destination with strong demand from US buyers. Prices have risen sharply since 2020. Limited road infrastructure keeps supply constrained.

Central Valley (Atenas, Grecia, San Ramón)

  • Homes: $150,000–$350,000

The Central Valley offers the best climate in the country (spring-like year-round at 3,000–4,000 feet elevation), lower prices, and proximity to San José. Popular with retirees who want to avoid beach-town heat and prices.

Escazú and Santa Ana (San José suburbs)

  • Condos and homes: $200,000–$500,000

The most upscale suburbs in the metro area. Modern infrastructure, international schools, hospitals, malls. Appeals to families and professionals who need urban amenities.

Ownership Structures: Personal Name vs. Corporation vs. Trust

When you buy property in Costa Rica, you have three main options for how to hold title. Each has trade-offs.

1. Direct deed in your personal name (escritura)

The simplest option. The notary prepares the deed in your name, registers it at the Registro Nacional, and you are the owner. No ongoing corporate filings, no annual corporate tax, no complexity. This is the right choice for most individual buyers purchasing a single home or condo.

2. Costa Rican corporation (Sociedad Anónima or S.R.L.)

Many attorneys will recommend forming a Costa Rican corporation (S.A. or S.R.L.) to hold the property. This was extremely popular before 2012 because corporations could hold property anonymously and transfer ownership simply by selling the shares (avoiding transfer tax). The landscape has changed:

  • Since 2012, all corporations must be registered with the beneficial owner disclosed.
  • Inactive corporations pay an annual corporate tax of approximately $120/year [verify Q3 2026].
  • Active corporations have higher filing requirements and costs.
  • Formation cost: approximately $1,000–$2,000 in legal fees.

A corporate structure still makes sense if you are holding multiple properties, plan to bring in partners, want liability separation between assets, or are running a short-term rental business. For a single personal residence, the added cost and complexity are usually not justified.

3. Fideicomiso (trust)

A trust is used almost exclusively in the Maritime Zone, where foreigners cannot hold concessions directly. The trust is managed by a licensed Costa Rican bank or trust company, with the foreign buyer as the beneficiary. Outside the Maritime Zone, trusts are rarely necessary.

Our recommendation: Direct deed for most buyers. Corporation if you are holding two or more properties or operating a rental business. Trust only if you are buying in the Maritime Zone.

The Buying Process Step by Step

The property buying process in Costa Rica is straightforward by Latin American standards, but it is not identical to buying in the US or Canada. Here is the typical sequence from start to finish.

Step 1: Find the property and make an offer

Most transactions begin with a verbal offer, followed by a written offer letter. There is no MLS in Costa Rica — properties are listed across multiple platforms (Encuentra24, Point2, individual agent websites) and many are marketed informally. Working with a local agent who knows the area is important, but note that Costa Rica does not license real estate agents the way the US does. Anyone can call themselves an agent. Ask for references and verify their track record.

Step 2: Due diligence

This is the most critical phase. Your attorney should conduct:

  • Title search at the Registro Nacional: Confirms the seller is the legal owner, checks for liens, mortgages, encumbrances, and annotations.
  • Survey review (plano catastrado): Every property should have a registered survey map. If the survey is outdated or missing, a new one must be commissioned by a licensed surveyor (topographer). Cost: $500–$2,000 depending on the size and terrain.
  • Municipal verification: Confirm that the property complies with local zoning, that property taxes are current, and that there are no pending municipal liens.
  • Water letter (carta de agua): Confirms that the property has access to municipal water or a registered water concession. In rural areas, water availability is not guaranteed. Do not skip this step.
  • Environmental review: For properties near rivers, forests, or protected areas, check for environmental restrictions (SETENA/SINAC regulations).
  • Occupancy check: Verify that no one is living on or using the land who might claim squatter rights (see Pitfalls section).

Step 3: Sales agreement (opción de compra-venta)

Once due diligence is satisfactory, both parties sign a purchase agreement. This typically includes:

  • Purchase price and payment terms
  • Deposit amount (typically 10% of the purchase price, held in escrow)
  • Conditions that must be met before closing
  • Closing date (usually 30–60 days from signing)
  • Penalties for breach by either party

Use an escrow service for the deposit. Costa Rica has reputable escrow companies, and some US title companies (like Stewart Title Latin America) offer escrow services for Costa Rican transactions.

Step 4: Hire a notary (notario público)

In Costa Rica, a notary is a licensed attorney with the additional authority to notarize documents. The notary prepares the transfer deed (escritura de traspaso), ensures all documents are in order, collects and distributes funds, and files the transfer with the Registro Nacional. The notary typically represents the transaction, not a single party — so both buyer and seller should have their own independent attorney for advice.

Step 5: Closing

At closing, the notary reads the deed to both parties, both sign, and the notary files the escritura at the Registro Nacional. Registration typically takes 2–4 weeks. Once registered, the buyer is the legal owner.

Timeline and costs

Total timeline from accepted offer to registered deed: 30–60 days in a straightforward transaction. Complex deals (corporate transfers, Maritime Zone concessions, title issues) can take longer.

Closing costs typically run 3.5–4.5% of the sale price, broken down approximately as:

  • Transfer tax: 1.5%
  • Documentary stamps and registration fees: ~0.5%
  • Notary/legal fees: 1–1.5%
  • Registro Nacional registration: ~0.5%

These costs are sometimes split between buyer and seller, but this is negotiable. Budget 3–4% on the buyer's side to be safe.

Taxes on Property in Costa Rica

Costa Rica's property tax burden is among the lowest in the Western Hemisphere. Here is what you will owe.

Annual property tax (impuesto sobre bienes inmuebles)

The annual property tax rate is 0.25% of the registered fiscal value. The fiscal value is determined by the municipality and is typically well below market value, though Costa Rica has been working to close that gap through periodic revaluations. Paid quarterly to the local municipality.

For context: a property with a fiscal value of $300,000 would owe $750 per year in property tax. Compare that to 1–3% rates common in the US.

Capital gains tax

As of 2019, Costa Rica imposes a 15% capital gains tax on profits from the sale of real estate. This applies to the gain (sale price minus purchase price and documented improvement costs). Prior to 2019, there was no capital gains tax on property sales. If you purchased before the law took effect, the cost basis is the property's fiscal value as of the law's effective date.

Luxury home tax (impuesto solidario)

Properties valued above a threshold (approximately $240,000 as of 2026 — this figure is adjusted annually for inflation [verify Q3 2026]) are subject to an additional luxury tax. The rate is progressive:

  • 0.25% on value from the threshold to approximately $560,000
  • 0.30% on value from ~$560,000 to ~$840,000
  • 0.35% on value from ~$840,000 to ~$1,120,000
  • 0.40–0.55% on value above ~$1,120,000

[Threshold amounts are adjusted annually by Costa Rica's tax authority. Verify the current brackets with a local tax advisor.]

Inheritance tax

Costa Rica does not impose an inheritance tax. Property passes to heirs without a death tax. However, the probate process can be slow (6–18 months), so estate planning with a local attorney is advisable.

Corporate tax (if using an S.A.)

If you hold property through a corporation, the corporation must pay an annual minimum corporate tax of approximately $120/year for inactive companies. Active corporations (generating rental income, for example) have standard corporate income tax obligations. [Verify current minimum tax amount with local counsel.]

US tax obligations

US citizens and permanent residents must report worldwide income, including rental income from Costa Rica, on their US tax return. If you hold property through a foreign corporation, you may have additional reporting requirements (Form 5471). If you have financial accounts in Costa Rica exceeding $10,000 in aggregate, you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114). FATCA reporting may also apply. Consult a US tax professional familiar with international holdings.

Residency Visas for Costa Rica

An important point that many buyers miss: owning property in Costa Rica does not grant you residency. You can own property as a tourist, but if you want to live in the country full-time, you need a residency visa. Here are the main options.

Rentista visa

Requires proof of stable, unearned income of at least $2,500 per month for two years ($60,000 total). This can be demonstrated through a bank deposit of $60,000 in a Costa Rican bank, pension income, investment returns, or a combination. Grants temporary residency for two years, renewable, and convertible to permanent residency after three years.

Pensionado visa

For retirees receiving a pension of at least $1,000 per month from a government or private pension source. This is the most common visa for US and Canadian retirees. Same timeline: temporary residency for two years, then permanent residency eligibility.

Inversionista (investor) visa

Requires an investment of at least $150,000 in Costa Rica. This can be in real estate, a business, or other qualified investments. The investment must be registered with immigration (DGME). This is the visa most relevant to property buyers, as your property purchase can qualify if it meets the threshold.

Timeline and process

Residency applications are processed by the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería (DGME). Typical processing time is 3–6 months, though delays are common. You will need:

  • Authenticated birth certificate (apostilled)
  • Police clearance from your home country (apostilled)
  • Proof of income or investment
  • Costa Rican consular registration
  • Local attorney to file the application

Temporary residency is granted for two years. After that, you can apply for permanent residency. Costa Rican citizenship is available after seven years of legal residency (five years for citizens of Spain and other Ibero-American countries).

While your application is pending, you can remain in the country legally. Temporary and permanent residents can work in Costa Rica, which is not the case for tourists.

Pitfalls and Risks to Avoid

Costa Rica is a safe and transparent market by Latin American standards, but there are specific traps that catch foreign buyers regularly. Here are the ones that matter most.

Maritime Zone violations

This is the single most common and expensive mistake. The first 200 meters from the high-tide line is regulated under the Maritime Zone Law. The first 50 meters is public land — no one can own or build on it. The next 150 meters can only be leased via concession. If someone tries to sell you "titled property" in the Maritime Zone, walk away. There are cases of buyers paying hundreds of thousands of dollars for properties that turned out to be on public land with no valid concession. Always verify the property's location relative to the high-tide line using the official plano catastrado and a surveyor's assessment.

Squatter rights (precarismo)

Costa Rican law provides protections for people who have occupied land openly and continuously for an extended period (typically 10+ years). If someone is living on or farming the land you want to buy, they may have a legal claim. This does not mean you cannot buy the property, but it means you need to verify occupancy status during due diligence. Your attorney should conduct a physical inspection of the property, not just a registry search.

Water access

In many rural and coastal areas, municipal water service is not guaranteed. Some properties rely on private wells, shared springs, or water cooperatives (ASADAS). Before purchasing, obtain a carta de agua (water availability letter) from the local water provider confirming that the property can receive service. Building a home on a lot without confirmed water access is one of the most common and costly errors for foreign buyers.

Unregistered roads and easements

Some properties are accessed via roads that are not officially registered, or that cross private land without a formal easement. This can create access problems down the road (literally). Verify that all access roads are either public roads or have registered easements (servidumbre) filed at the Registro Nacional.

Title insurance

Title insurance is not standard in Costa Rica the way it is in the US, but it is available through companies like Stewart Title Latin America and First American Title. Given the risks of Maritime Zone issues, unregistered liens, and boundary disputes, title insurance is a worthwhile investment. Cost is typically 0.5–1% of the purchase price for a one-time policy.

Buying an existing corporation

If the property is held in a corporation (S.A.), you may be offered the option to buy the corporation's shares rather than transferring the deed. This avoids the 1.5% transfer tax, but it also means you inherit all of the corporation's liabilities — including unknown debts, tax obligations, and legal claims. Never buy a corporation you did not form yourself without a thorough audit of the entity's history, financials, and legal standing. In most cases, it is safer to form a new corporation and transfer the deed.

Choosing the wrong attorney

Use a Costa Rican attorney who is licensed, experienced with real estate transactions involving foreign buyers, and — critically — independent from the seller and the listing agent. Dual representation (where the seller's attorney also represents you) is common but creates obvious conflicts of interest. Budget $1,500–$3,000 for independent legal representation.

Best Areas for Investment in 2026

Where you buy depends on what you want — rental income, personal use, appreciation, or some combination. Here is a quick overview of the main regions.

Guanacaste Coast (Pacific Northwest)

The most established tourism and expat market. Strong short-term rental demand, good airport access (Liberia), and the most liquid resale market. Tamarindo, Playas del Coco, and the Papagayo Peninsula are the hubs. Prices are the highest on the Pacific side, but rental yields can be strong (5–8% gross) for well-located condos. [Source: local property management data, Q1 2026]

Central Valley

Best value for full-time living. Atenas, Grecia, and San Ramón offer mild climates, low prices, and easy access to San José. Not a strong rental market, but excellent for retirees who want lower costs and a less touristy environment. The Central Valley also has the country's best healthcare infrastructure.

Southern Pacific (Dominical, Uvita, Ojochal)

The emerging market. Lower prices than Guanacaste or Manuel Antonio, dramatic scenery (the Whale's Tail at Marino Ballena National Park), and a growing international community. Infrastructure is improving but still behind the northern beaches. Good for buyers who want to get in before prices climb further.

Caribbean Coast (Puerto Viejo, Cahuita)

A different feel from the Pacific side — Afro-Caribbean culture, reggae, reef breaks, and jungle. Lower prices overall but also more Maritime Zone complexity (many beachfront properties are on concession land). Less institutional investment, fewer large developments. Appeals to buyers looking for an alternative lifestyle rather than a conventional expat experience.

The Bottom Line

Costa Rica offers something rare: a stable, foreigner-friendly property market in a country where the rule of law actually functions. You can buy property in your own name with the same rights as a citizen. Closing costs are reasonable. Property taxes are among the lowest in the hemisphere. And the lifestyle — climate, nature, healthcare, safety — is why over 100,000 North American and European expats call it home.

But you need to do your homework. Hire an independent attorney. Run full due diligence. Stay away from Maritime Zone properties unless you have expert guidance. Get the water letter. Understand your tax obligations in both Costa Rica and your home country.

If you do those things, buying property in Costa Rica is one of the more straightforward international real estate transactions you can make. The country has been welcoming foreign buyers for decades, the legal framework is well-established, and the market is deep enough that you can find quality properties at a range of price points.

Start with a scouting trip. Visit at least two or three areas. Talk to expats who have already bought. And when you are ready, bring in a local attorney before you sign anything.
