Costa Rica Property Buying Costs, Taxes and Rules: 2026 Guide

By Charles Brossy May 6, 2026 12 min read
Published May 6, 2026 · Last updated October 19, 2026

In This Guide

Costa Rica draws foreign buyers for reasons that are easy to verify. The US State Department describes it as the oldest continuous democracy in Latin America, its constitution has abolished the army as a permanent institution since 1949, and Numbeo's crowd-sourced data currently puts everyday consumer prices about 18% below the United States (excluding rent), with rents about 44% lower.

But buying property abroad is not the same as browsing Zillow. Costa Rica has its own legal system, its own closing process, and a handful of traps that can cost you serious money if you walk in without doing your homework. This guide focuses on what a purchase actually costs in 2026 — closing costs, annual taxes, the luxury home tax, capital gains on resale — plus who can buy, how the process works and where residency fits in.

Every figure below was checked against an official or established source on October 19, 2026; the sources are listed at the end. Tax thresholds in Costa Rica are set in colones and updated every year, so we quote them in colones rather than converting at a rate that will be out of date next month. Where you need a local attorney, we tell you.

Can Foreigners Buy Property in Costa Rica?

Yes. Article 19 of the Costa Rican Constitution gives foreigners the same individual and social rights as Costa Ricans, subject to the exceptions set by the Constitution and the law, and Article 45 declares private property inviolable. The State Department's 2025 Investment Climate Statement confirms that foreigners are subject to the same land acquisition laws as Costa Ricans, apart from concessions in the Maritime Zone. You do not need residency or a local partner to buy titled property in your own name.

The major exception is the Maritime Zone (Zona Marítimo Terrestre, or ZMT), created by Law No. 6043. Almost all beachfront is state property for 200 meters inland from the mean high-tide line, with a few exceptions for properties duly registered before the law was enacted:

  • Public Zone (first 50 meters): Public land. No one can occupy it or build on it.
  • Restricted Zone (next 150 meters): Also owned by the state. It cannot be bought; the municipality grants concessions, which are leases for set periods and specified uses such as tourism or vacation homes. A concession is a lease from the state, not ownership.

Under Article 47 of the Maritime Zone Law (Law 6043), concessions cannot be granted to foreigners who have lived in Costa Rica for less than five years, or to companies more than 50% foreign-owned; the State Department and the Tico Times both flag this limit. Buyers are sometimes offered structures designed to work around this rule. Treat any such proposal as a matter for independent specialist counsel, not something to accept on a seller's or agent's word.

Outside the Maritime Zone, if the property has a registered title in the Registro Nacional, a foreigner can buy it outright on the same terms as a citizen.

What Property Costs and Where Buyers Look

We do not publish neighborhood price ranges on this page because we could not verify current asking or sale prices for each area from a reliable source on the date of this update. Listings are spread across portals such as Encuentra24 and individual agency sites. Before you budget, pull current comparable listings for the exact area and property type, and ask your attorney what similar properties have actually been registered for.

The main areas foreign buyers consider are:

  • Guanacaste coast (Tamarindo, Playas del Coco, Flamingo, the Papagayo Peninsula): the most established tourism and vacation-rental area, served by Liberia's international airport (LIR).
  • Central Pacific and Southern Pacific (Manuel Antonio, Dominical, Uvita, Ojochal): beach towns closer to San José, with more limited infrastructure the further south you go.
  • Nosara and Sámara on the Nicoya Peninsula: surf and wellness towns where road access limits how quickly supply can grow.
  • Central Valley (Atenas, Grecia, San Ramón): cooler, higher-elevation towns within reach of San José, popular with full-time residents and retirees.
  • Escazú and Santa Ana: San José's upscale suburbs, with international schools, private hospitals and shopping.
  • Caribbean coast (Puerto Viejo, Cahuita): a different culture and pace, but many beachfront lots sit on Maritime Zone concession land, so due diligence matters even more.

Ownership Structures: Personal Name vs. Corporation

When you buy property in Costa Rica, the two common options are to hold title in your own name or through a Costa Rican company. Each has trade-offs.

1. Direct deed in your personal name

The simplest option. The notary prepares the deed in your name and records it at the Registro Nacional. There are no company filings or annual company tax. For most individual buyers purchasing a single home or condo, this is the straightforward choice.

2. Costa Rican corporation (S.A. or S.R.L.)

Holding property through a company was once popular partly because ownership could change hands by selling shares. That advantage has narrowed, and companies now carry ongoing obligations:

  • Beneficial ownership reporting: Law 9416 created the Registry of Transparency and Final Beneficiaries, filed with the Central Bank (BCCR). Companies domiciled in Costa Rica must report shareholders and beneficial owners holding 15% or more of capital, annually and when ownership changes.
  • Annual tax on legal entities (Law 9428): For 2026 the base salary used to calculate it stays at ₡462,200. A company not registered as an income-tax contributor pays 15% of that (₡69,330); a company with no economic activity, zero declared income or gross income below 120 base salaries pays 25% (₡115,550). Higher rates apply to companies with more income. The tax is owed whether or not the company is active.
  • Transfer tax still applies to share sales: the 1.5% transfer tax is also triggered by an indirect transfer when control of the entity that holds the property changes (see below).

A company can still make sense if you hold several properties, have partners, or run a rental business, but for a single personal residence the cost and paperwork are often not justified. Discuss it with your attorney and your home-country tax adviser before deciding.

The Buying Process Step by Step

The sequence below is the typical path for a resale purchase. Your attorney will adapt it to the property.

Step 1: Find the property and make an offer

Offers are usually made in writing through the agent or the seller's representative. Vet any agent by asking for references from past buyers and checking their track record, and keep your own attorney involved from the first written offer.

Step 2: Due diligence

This is the most important phase. Your attorney should check:

  • Title at the Registro Nacional: who the registered owner is, and any mortgages, liens, annotations or encumbrances.
  • Registered survey (plano catastrado): that the boundaries on the ground match the registered plan, and, near the coast, where the property sits relative to the Maritime Zone.
  • Municipal status: zoning, and whether property taxes are paid up.
  • Water availability: construction permits require a water availability letter from the local ASADA (community water association) or from AyA, the national water utility. Get it before any money moves.
  • Access and easements: that access roads are public or covered by a registered easement. The State Department notes that the right to use traditional paths can be used to obtain court-ordered easements over titled land, and that easement disputes are common where beach access is involved.
  • Occupancy: a physical inspection to confirm no one is living on or using the land (see Pitfalls).

Step 3: Purchase agreement and escrow

Once due diligence is satisfactory, the parties sign an option or purchase agreement setting the price, deposit, conditions, closing date and penalties for breach. Deposits and purchase funds should go through an escrow agent registered with SUGEF, the financial regulator; escrow typically costs about 0.25% or a flat fee of around $1,000 and should not be waived.

Step 4: The notary and the deed

In Costa Rica most attorneys may act as notaries public, with the power to formally register changes in title. The notary prepares the transfer deed, collects the taxes and fees, and records the deed at the Registro Nacional. According to the State Department, the Registry has no legal authority to question notarial acts beyond procedural formalities, which is why it matters that you have your own independent attorney rather than relying on the seller's.

Step 5: Closing and registration

Both parties sign the deed before the notary, who then presents it for registration. Ownership is secured once the deed is recorded in your name. Complex deals — corporate transfers, Maritime Zone concessions, title defects — take longer than a clean resale.

Costa Rica Property Buying Costs in 2026

For a straightforward residential purchase, closing costs run roughly 3.5–5% of the purchase price, more with bank financing or corporate structures. The main components:

  • Transfer tax: 1.5% of the sale price or the property's tax (fiscal) value, whichever is greater.
  • Documentary stamps and Registro Nacional recording fees: roughly 0.5–0.8%.
  • Notary fees: set by the Colegio de Abogados fee schedule, generally 1–2% of the price, plus VAT on the fee.
  • Escrow: about 0.25% or a flat fee near $1,000.

No law fixes who pays what. Market custom is to split these costs between buyer and seller, but it is negotiable, so settle the split in the purchase agreement. Due diligence, survey and independent legal fees come on top; ask for written quotes before you commit.

Taxes on Property in Costa Rica

Annual property tax

The national property tax rate is 0.25% of the appraised value registered with the municipality where the property sits; each municipality handles appraisals. As an illustration, a registered value of $300,000 would mean $750 a year.

Luxury home tax (impuesto solidario)

Homes whose construction and fixed installations have a fiscal value above an exempt amount also pay the solidarity tax on luxury homes (land is left out of that threshold test). For 2026, Decree 45358-H (published December 19, 2025) set the exempt amount at ₡143,000,000. Once a home is over it, the tax is charged on the whole fiscal value of the home including land, not just the part above the threshold, using a progressive scale (Law 8683, articles 4–5):

  • Up to ₡359 million: 0.25%
  • ₡359 million to ₡720 million: 0.30%
  • ₡720 million to ₡1,081 million: 0.35%
  • ₡1,081 million to ₡1,443 million: 0.40%
  • ₡1,443 million to ₡1,800 million: 0.45%
  • ₡1,800 million to ₡2,162 million: 0.50%
  • Above ₡2,162 million: 0.55%

The amounts are updated each year in line with consumer prices. The tax is paid annually, with the deadline in mid-January, and the return is filed every three years; anyone who bought a taxable home during the previous year must also file form D-174. Ask your attorney or accountant to check whether your home is above the threshold.

Rental income

Individuals renting out property in Costa Rica pay 15% on rental income under the real estate capital income rules, after a standard deduction of 15% of gross income, according to the Ministry of Finance (Hacienda).

Capital gains tax when you sell

Since July 1, 2019 (Law 9635), gains on the sale of real estate are taxed at 15% of the gain. For property acquired before July 1, 2019, the seller can instead opt, on the first sale, to pay 2.25% of the sale price. The sale of your habitual home is exempt, and Hacienda says the exemption can also apply where the home is held in a company if it is genuinely used as the owners' residence. When the seller is a non-resident, the buyer must withhold 2.5% of the price as an advance payment. Property used in a business is taxed under the business income rules instead.

Inheritance and gift tax

Costa Rica has no inheritance tax and no gift tax, and Hacienda lists inheritances, legacies and donations as exempt from capital gains tax. Estate planning with a local attorney is still worthwhile, because Costa Rican property passes under Costa Rican procedures.

US tax obligations

US citizens and green-card holders report worldwide income, including Costa Rican rental income, on their US return. If your foreign financial accounts together exceed $10,000 at any time during the year, you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114). Owning a stake in a foreign corporation can trigger Form 5471. Work with a US tax professional who handles international holdings.

Residency: Owning Property Is Not a Visa

Owning property in Costa Rica does not give you residency. You can own property as a visitor, but to live in the country full-time you need a residence permit from the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería (DGME). The main categories for buyers:

Rentista

Proof of at least $2,500 a month of stable income for at least two years, guaranteed by a bank, or a $60,000 deposit in an approved Costa Rican bank. One amount covers the family.

Pensionado

A lifetime pension of at least $1,000 a month. It must be a pension; investment or rental income does not count.

Inversionista (investor)

The investor category traditionally required $200,000. Law 9996 cut the minimum to $150,000, including qualifying real estate, but only for the period the law sets: benefits were available to people who applied during the law's first five years from 2021. According to the Tico Times, that five-year incentive period ended on July 14, 2026. Whether the $150,000 investor minimum lapsed with it and reverted to $200,000 has not been officially clarified, so confirm with DGME or an immigration attorney which threshold applies before you size a purchase around residency.

Process, work rights and citizenship

Applications need an apostilled (or consularized) birth certificate and criminal-record certificate, plus proof of income or investment; income and police certificates are generally valid for six months. Temporary residence cards are renewed every two years, and temporary residents can seek permanent residence after three years. Rentistas and pensionados cannot work as employees, though they can own a business. Under Article 14 of the Constitution, naturalization requires seven years of official residence, or five years for Central Americans, Spaniards and Ibero-Americans by birth, plus other legal requirements.

Pitfalls and Risks to Avoid

Maritime Zone mistakes

The first 50 meters from the mean high-tide line is public and cannot be built on; the next 150 meters can only be held by concession. Be very skeptical of beachfront marketed as "titled" within 200 meters of the high-tide line, since only a few pre-law registrations are exceptions. Confirm the location on the registered survey with your own surveyor and attorney.

Title fraud

Title recording is mandatory and most land has clear title, but the 2024 "Madre Patria" case, in which a criminal network allegedly used notaries to change deeds and sell absent owners' properties, exposed the Registry's vulnerability. The Registro Nacional runs an electronic alert service, costing about $15 a year, that emails owners when a deed affecting their property is presented. Sign up as soon as you close.

Squatters

Costa Rican law allows long-time occupants to take legal possession of land if the owner does not oppose them, although the State Department notes the Supreme Court has in recent years refused title to squatters on already-titled land. Inspect the property in person, not just the registry, and keep watch over land you own but do not live on.

Water and access

Without a water availability letter from the ASADA or AyA, you cannot get a construction permit, so a cheap lot without confirmed water can be unbuildable. Likewise, check that access is via a public road or a registered easement.

Buying an existing company

If the seller holds the property in a company, you may be offered the shares instead of a deed transfer. This does not avoid the 1.5% transfer tax, which also applies when control of the entity holding the property changes. It does mean you inherit the company's history, including unknown debts, tax obligations and legal claims. Never buy an existing company without a full audit; forming a new company and transferring the deed is often cleaner.

Using the seller's attorney

Because notaries can register title changes and the Registry cannot second-guess them, hire your own licensed attorney, independent from the seller and the listing agent, before you sign anything.

The Bottom Line

Costa Rica lets foreigners buy titled property in their own name on the same terms as citizens. Budget roughly 3.5–5% for closing costs, 0.25% a year in property tax, and check whether the luxury home tax applies. Plan your exit too: 15% on the gain (or the 2.25% option for pre-2019 property), with your habitual home exempt.

Do the homework. Hire an independent attorney, run full due diligence, get the water letter, stay away from Maritime Zone deals without specialist advice, and register for the Registry's title alerts. If residency is part of the plan, confirm the current investor threshold with DGME before you buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the closing costs when buying property in Costa Rica in 2026?

For a straightforward residential purchase, roughly 3.5–5% of the price: a 1.5% transfer tax on the higher of the sale price or fiscal value, about 0.5–0.8% in stamps and registration fees, and notary fees of generally 1–2% plus VAT. Buyer and seller customarily split the costs, but the split is negotiable.

Does buying property in Costa Rica give me residency?

No. Ownership alone does not grant residency. The investor category traditionally requires $200,000; Law 9996 lowered it to $150,000 for applications during its first five years from 2021, a period the Tico Times reports ended in July 2026. Whether $200,000 now applies again has not been officially clarified, so confirm the threshold with Costa Rica's immigration directorate (DGME) before buying.

How much capital gains tax do I pay when I sell property in Costa Rica?

15% of the gain. For property acquired before July 1, 2019, you can opt to pay 2.25% of the sale price on the first sale instead. The sale of your habitual home is exempt, and if you are a non-resident the buyer withholds 2.5% of the price as an advance payment.
