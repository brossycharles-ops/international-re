How to Buy Property in Costa Rica as a Foreigner (2026)

By Charles Brossy  ·  April 29, 2026  ·  Updated October 20, 2026  ·  14 min read

The short answer: foreigners can buy property in Costa Rica freely, easily, and with the same rights as Costa Rican citizens. There is no foreign ownership restriction, no quota, no requirement to be a resident. But there are a few traps — the maritime zone law being the biggest — and the legal process has specific steps worth understanding before you wire any money.

This guide covers the complete process: legal rights, how title works, what the maritime zone means for beach properties, closing costs, the typical timeline, what to watch for, and where prices actually stand in 2026. Every figure was re-checked against official or established sources on October 20, 2026; they are listed at the end.

1. Foreign Property Rights in Costa Rica

Article 19 of the Costa Rican Constitution gives foreigners the same individual and social rights as Costa Ricans, with the exceptions set by the Constitution and the law. In practice, foreigners can buy titled property on the same terms as Costa Rican citizens: the US State Department's 2025 Investment Climate Statement says foreigners are subject to the same land acquisition rules as Costa Ricans apart from concessions in the Maritime Zone. This is unusually strong compared to many countries — Mexico, Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Vietnam all have significant restrictions on foreign ownership that Costa Rica simply does not have.

Key points:

  • No nationality restrictions. Citizens of any country can purchase.
  • No residency requirement. You can buy as a tourist.
  • No cap on the percentage of a property a foreigner can own.
  • No special permits or government approvals required for standard transactions.
  • You can hold property in your own name or through a Costa Rican corporation (S.A. or S.R.L.).
  • Buying property does not by itself give you residency (see the FAQ below).

The exception — and it's a significant one — is beach and waterfront property subject to the Maritime Zone Law.

2. The Maritime Zone Trap

This is the single most common source of costly mistakes for foreign buyers in Costa Rica. Understanding it clearly before you look at beach properties is essential.

The Ley sobre la Zona Marítimo Terrestre (ZMT, Law 6043 of 1977) covers a 200-meter strip along both coasts, measured from the ordinary high-tide line, and divides it into two zones:

The Two Coastal Zones

Public Zone (Zona Pública) — 0 to 50 meters from high tide: This land is entirely public. It cannot be owned by anyone, bought, sold, or titled, and no construction is allowed. Any "sale" of land in the public zone is legally meaningless. Do not buy anything in this strip.

Restricted Zone (Zona Restringida) — 50 to 200 meters from high tide: This land also belongs to the state and can only be held via a concession granted by the local municipality, for a term of 5 to 20 years (Article 48). The concession is a lease, not ownership. And here's the critical part: Article 47 bars concessions for foreigners who have not lived in Costa Rica for at least five years, for companies domiciled abroad or set up in Costa Rica by foreigners, for companies with bearer shares, and for entities more than 50% foreign-owned.

What this means in practice:

  • Many beachfront and near-beach properties in Costa Rica are concession land, not titled land. The State Department notes only a few exceptions for properties duly registered before the law was enacted.
  • Buying a company that holds a concession is not a workaround: Article 47 also says companies holding concessions cannot transfer shares or quotas to foreigners, and transfers that break this rule have no validity. Treat any "nominee" structure as a serious legal risk.
  • Transferring a concession requires the approval of the municipality and of the Costa Rican Tourism Institute (ICT) or the land institute, as applicable (Article 45).
  • Land beyond the 200-meter strip is ordinary titled land. This is where most foreign buyers should focus.

Bottom line: Always get your own Costa Rican property attorney to confirm the exact legal status of any coastal property before signing anything. The cost of that check is small compared to the risk.

3. Step-by-Step Buying Process

Step 1: Engage a local attorney (abogado)

Costa Rica uses a notarial system — a notary-attorney (notario público) must handle the transfer deed. Most Costa Rican attorneys can act as notaries and register title changes, and the State Department notes the Registry has no legal authority to question notarial acts beyond procedural formalities. That is why you need your own attorney, not just the seller's. Notary fees follow the official fee schedule (arancel, Decree 41457-JP) plus 13% VAT; the Tico Times put them at 1–2% of the price as of September 2026. Ask for a written quote of fees and expenses before you start.

Step 2: Title search (estudio de título)

Your attorney runs a full search through the Registro Nacional (National Registry) to confirm:

  • The seller is the legal owner
  • There are no liens, mortgages, or encumbrances on the property
  • Property taxes are current (check the Municipalidad records)
  • The property is not in a flood zone, protected area, or maritime zone
A certified copy of the registry entry (certificación literal) and the cadastral plan can be obtained online the same day; the World Bank's last benchmark recorded this step as taking less than a day. How long the full study takes depends on the property.

Step 3: Survey (plano catastrado)

Check that the registered survey plan (plano catastrado) matches the physical boundaries on the ground. If it doesn't exist or is outdated, you'll need a licensed surveyor (topógrafo) to prepare a new one; get a quote, since cost depends on the property. Also confirm water availability (a construction permit needs a water availability letter from the local ASADA or from AyA), zoning (uso de suelo), legal access and whether existing buildings were permitted.

Step 4: Due diligence and negotiation

Once title is confirmed clean, negotiate terms and put them in a Promesa de Compraventa or option agreement (opción de compra). This document sets out price, deposit, conditions, closing date and penalties for breach. The deposit amount is negotiable, so settle it in writing. Send deposits and purchase funds through an escrow agent registered with SUGEF, the financial regulator, never directly to the seller. This is a binding contract once signed — have your attorney review it before you sign.

Step 5: Closing (escritura pública)

The notary-attorney prepares the transfer deed (escritura). Both parties (or their authorized representatives via power of attorney) sign before the notary. The deed is then submitted to the National Registry for recording.

Step 6: National Registry recording

After submission, a registrar reviews the deed and, if it complies, records the transfer to the new owner; a deed with problems is returned to the notary for correction and refiling. In the World Bank's Doing Business 2020 benchmark (May 2019 data, clean title in San José), registration took 7 days and the five official transfer steps took 11 days in total. No newer official figure is published, so treat this as a benchmark, not a promise. Your ownership is secure against third parties once the deed is recorded in your name; then subscribe to the Registry's title alert service, which the State Department puts at about $15 a year.

Step 7: Utility and municipal transfers

Once title is in your name, transfer utilities (ESPH, AyA, CNFL depending on location), update property tax records at the Municipalidad, and if applicable, set up the HOA account.

4. Closing Costs and Taxes

As of September 2026, the Tico Times put total closing costs for a straightforward purchase at roughly 3.5–5% of the purchase price, toward the upper end with bank financing or a corporate structure. By law the transfer tax is split equally between seller and buyer; how the other costs are split is custom, not law, so settle it in the purchase agreement.

The stamp rates are those itemised in the World Bank's Doing Business 2020 profile, alongside small fixed fees such as the bar association and fiscal stamps; the Tico Times puts stamps and Registry recording together at roughly 0.5–0.8%. Because the transfer tax and stamps are charged on the higher of the price and the registered value, under-declaring the price in the deed does not reliably reduce them. Buying the seller's company instead of the property also triggers the 1.5% transfer tax, since Law 6999 treats a change of control of a property-holding company as an indirect transfer.

Annual property taxes: Costa Rica's property tax (impuesto sobre bienes inmuebles, Law 7509) is 0.25% of the appraised value registered with the municipality where the property sits. A property registered at $400,000 pays $1,000 a year.

Luxury home tax (impuesto solidario, Law 8683): for 2026, Decree 45358-H set the exempt amount at ₡143 million, measured on the fiscal value of the construction. Above that, the land is added and the tax is charged on the whole value of home plus land, at progressive rates from 0.25% (up to ₡359 million) to 0.55% (above ₡2,162 million). The amounts are updated each December and the tax is due by January 15.

Capital gains when you sell: since July 1, 2019 (Law 9635), gains are taxed at 15%. For property acquired before that date, the seller can opt to pay 2.25% of the sale price on the first sale instead. When the seller is a non-resident the buyer must withhold 2.5% of the price.

5. Typical Timeline

1
Attorney and title study: engage your own attorney-notary; registry certifications are available online the same day
2
Due diligence and agreement: survey, water, zoning and permits checked; sign the purchase agreement and pay the negotiated deposit into SUGEF-registered escrow
3
Deed preparation: notary prepares the transfer deed (escritura); remaining funds arranged in escrow
4
Closing: deed signed before the notary; transfer tax and stamps paid; balance released to seller
5
Registration: National Registry records the transfer (7 days in the World Bank's 2019-data benchmark)

The only official benchmark covers the transfer itself: 11 days for all five steps in the World Bank's Doing Business 2020 profile (May 2019 data). No official figure covers due diligence, negotiation or financing, so the full purchase varies from deal to deal.

6. 2026 Price Data by Market

Prices vary dramatically by region, and Costa Rica has no official house price index by town. The most recent published market snapshot we could source is a Tico Times market outlook from January 2026, which put the national average at about $1,021 per square meter in 2025. These are the figures available as of October 2026; for any specific property, get comparable sales from a local attorney or appraiser before you offer.

Median home value in early 2025, down from peaks above $900,000 in 2024 as the luxury segment corrected. Tamarindo, Flamingo and Playa Hermosa: rental returns of about 5–8%; outlook of 3–7% annual gains.

Central Valley (San José, Escazú, Santa Ana, Heredia): appreciation outlook of 4–6% a year, with steady demand from families, professionals and expats. No sourced price range by unit type.

Jacó / Pacific Mid

$1,500–2,000 / m²

Central Pacific prices per square meter, with 6–8% gains possible in sought-after parts. Short-term rental oversupply in places like Jacó has pushed some owners toward longer stays or mixed models.

Caribbean Coast

Below Pacific levels

Puerto Viejo/Cahuita area and Limón Province: prices stay below Pacific levels, with slower progress. As on every coast, land within 200 meters of high tide is Maritime Zone land, so check status before you offer.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners buy property in Costa Rica?

Yes. Article 19 of the Constitution gives foreigners the same rights as Costa Ricans, with exceptions set by law. No residency is required and there are no nationality restrictions or ownership caps on titled property. The main exception is the Maritime Zone: concessions within 200 meters of the high-tide line are barred to foreigners with under five years of residence and to companies more than 50% foreign-owned.

Do I need to be a resident to buy property in Costa Rica?

No. You can buy titled property without residency. Buying does not by itself give you residency either: the investor (inversionista) category needs a qualifying investment and approval from the immigration directorate (DGME). Law 9996 set the minimum at $150,000 instead of the traditional $200,000, but its five-year window ended in July 2026 and, as of October 2026, it has not been officially clarified which threshold now applies, so confirm with DGME before buying. For the pensionado and rentista routes, see our guide to retiring in Costa Rica.

Do I need to form a company to buy property?

No. Titled property can be bought in your own name. Some buyers use a Costa Rican S.A. or S.R.L., but a company carries annual costs and filings, and selling the company's shares instead of the property still triggers the 1.5% transfer tax. A company does not get around the Maritime Zone rules: concessions cannot go to entities more than 50% foreign-owned, and concession holders cannot transfer shares to foreigners.

Can I get a mortgage as a foreigner?

It is difficult. According to the Tico Times (September 2026), Costa Rican banks generally lend only to legal residents, at loan-to-value ratios well below North American norms and with long approval times. Most foreign buyers pay cash or arrange financing in their home country.

Is title insurance necessary?

It is optional. Title recording is mandatory and most land has clear title, but the 2024 Madre Patria fraud case showed the Registry can be manipulated. Your own attorney's title study is the core protection. If you want a policy, ask a provider for a written quote. After closing, subscribe to the National Registry's title alert service, which the US State Department puts at about $15 a year.

What taxes do I pay when I sell?

Since July 1, 2019 (Law 9635), capital gains on real estate are taxed at 15% of the gain. For property acquired before July 1, 2019, the seller can instead choose to pay 2.25% of the sale price on the first sale. If the seller is a non-resident, the buyer must withhold 2.5% of the price. The 1.5% transfer tax is owed half by the seller and half by the buyer.
