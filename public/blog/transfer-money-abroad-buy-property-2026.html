How to Transfer Money Abroad to Buy Property in 2026

By Charles Brossy October 19, 2026 8 min read

To transfer money abroad to buy property, send it electronically, in as few large transfers as possible, from an account in your own name to the account your lawyer, notary or escrow agent gives you, and keep every bank record. Compare providers on the total amount the recipient gets, not the advertised fee, verify the wiring instructions by phone before you send, and follow the destination country’s rules: Mexico bans cash payments for real estate worth 8,025 UMA or more (about MXN 941,413 in 2026), and Colombia requires the money to come in through its regulated foreign-exchange market and be registered as foreign investment.

This guide is written for US-based buyers, since US federal rules give you specific protections on a transfer. The steps work the same way from anywhere. The money you send to close a purchase is often the largest payment you will ever make, and it is the step where small mistakes cost the most: a bad exchange rate on a $300,000 transfer, a forged email with new account numbers, or a missing registration form that makes it harder to get your money out when you sell.

Your Options: Bank Wire or Specialist Transfer Provider

There are two common ways to move a six-figure sum. A bank wire from your own bank usually goes over the SWIFT network, sometimes through one or more correspondent banks that may each take a fee along the way. A specialist money-transfer provider (a licensed non-bank remittance company) often converts the currency itself and pays out from a local account in the destination country.

The cost of either has two parts: the stated fee, and the exchange-rate margin, meaning the gap between the rate you get and the mid-market rate. On a large transfer the margin usually matters far more than a flat fee, so “no fee” offers can still be expensive. The cleanest way to compare is the figure US law makes providers show you before you pay.

What US Law Gives You on a Transfer

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s remittance transfer rule (Regulation E, subpart B) covers electronic transfers above $15 that a consumer in the US sends abroad for personal, family or household purposes. Transfers for business purposes are not covered, so if you buy through a company, these protections may not apply. Banks and other providers that send 500 or fewer remittance transfers a year are also outside the rule, so ask whether yours gives these disclosures. For covered transfers:

  1. Disclosure before you pay. The provider must show the transfer amount, its fees and taxes, the exchange rate and the “Total to Recipient” in the foreign currency.
  2. A receipt repeating those figures plus the “Date Available”, the date the money will reach the foreign country.
  3. A 30-minute cancellation window. You can cancel if the provider receives your request no later than 30 minutes after you pay (providers may allow longer), as long as the money hasn’t already been picked up or deposited. The refund, including fees, must be issued within three business days.
  4. Error resolution. If the recipient gets less than the disclosed Total to Recipient, that counts as an error the provider must investigate and fix, with limited exceptions such as lawful estimates and the receiving bank’s own disclosed fees.

Get a written quote from your bank and from at least one licensed specialist on the same day, and compare the Total to Recipient for the same amount.

Mexico: Wire It, Don’t Carry It

Article 32 of Mexico’s anti-money-laundering law (the Ley Federal para la Prevención e Identificación de Operaciones con Recursos de Procedencia Ilícita) prohibits paying for, or accepting payment for, the creation or transfer of rights over real estate in cash (bills and coins, in pesos or foreign currency, or precious metals) when the value is 8,025 times the daily UMA or more. That applies even if the cash goes through a bank. INEGI set the 2026 UMA at MXN 117.31 a day from 1 February 2026, which puts the threshold at about MXN 941,413, well below the price of most homes foreigners buy. The UMA is updated every 1 February, so the peso figure rises each year. In practice that means a bank transfer that leaves a paper trail. Colombia: Bring It In Through the Regulated Market and Register It

Colombia treats a non-resident’s purchase of a home as foreign direct investment. Decree 1068 of 2015 (article 2.17.2.2.1.2) lists “real estate located in the country, acquired by any title” as direct investment, whether bought outright or through a trust. In a 2019 opinion, Banco de la República confirmed two consequences. First, the investment must be registered under the bank’s foreign-exchange regulations (the opinion cited Circular DCIN-83, since replaced by newer circulars). Second, it is a mandatory foreign-exchange operation: the foreign currency must be transferred through a licensed foreign-exchange intermediary (in practice a Colombian bank) or a registered compensation account. The bank opinion adds that skipping registration does not affect your ownership of the property. Registration is what documents your right as a foreign investor to send the proceeds back out when you sell, so don’t let a seller talk you into an informal transfer.

Ask your Colombian lawyer to confirm the current version of the foreign-exchange declaration with the receiving bank before you send. For the wider buying process, see Can Foreigners Buy Property in Colombia? and our Medellín city guide.

A Step-by-Step Checklist for the Closing Transfer

  1. Get the receiving account in writing from your own lawyer or notary, not from the seller or an agent’s email. Ask what currency the account takes and whether the deed price must be paid in local currency.
  2. Confirm destination-country paperwork before you send: the foreign-investment declaration in Colombia, or the notary’s requirements for proof of payment in Mexico.
  3. Prepare proof of where the money came from (sale of a home, brokerage statements, payslips). Banks on both ends may ask, and a large unexplained transfer can be delayed while they check.
  4. Get two same-day quotes and compare Total to Recipient.
  5. Send a small test amount and have the recipient confirm it arrived before the main transfer.
  6. Call to verify the instructions, using a phone number you already had, immediately before the main wire.
  7. Keep the disclosure, receipt and SWIFT confirmation with your deed. You will want them when you sell.

The Biggest Risk: A Fake Email Changing the Account

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that scammers pose as a buyer’s agent or settlement company and email last-minute changes to wiring instructions, sometimes after breaking into a real estate professional’s email account. Its advice applies just as much to a closing in Panama City or Medellín: verify the account name and number in person or by a phone number you agreed on earlier, never by a number given in the email, and treat any change of instructions as suspect. If you have already wired money to a fraudster, contact your bank or transfer company immediately to try to stop or reverse it, then report it to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center. Money that has been converted and paid out abroad is very hard to recover.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I pay cash for a house in Mexico as a foreigner?

Not for most homes. Article 32 of Mexico's anti-money-laundering law prohibits paying in cash (bills and coins, pesos or foreign currency) for the transfer of real estate worth 8,025 UMA or more. With the 2026 UMA at MXN 117.31, that threshold is about MXN 941,413. Use a bank transfer instead.

Do I have to register a property purchase with Colombia's central bank?

Yes. Colombia treats a non-resident's purchase of a home as foreign direct investment. The foreign currency must be brought in through a licensed foreign-exchange intermediary or a compensation account, and the investment registered under Banco de la República's current foreign-exchange rules. Registration matters later, when you want to send sale proceeds back out.

Do I report a foreign house to the IRS?

Not just for owning it. The IRS says foreign real estate held directly is not reportable on the FBAR or on Form 8938. But a foreign bank account you open to pay bills must go on an FBAR if your foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any point in the year, and rental income or a gain on sale is taxable as usual.
