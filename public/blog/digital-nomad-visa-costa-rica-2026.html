Costa Rica Digital Nomad Visa 2026: Requirements, Cost & How to Apply

By Charles Brossy April 30, 2026 · Updated October 20, 2026 10 min read

Costa Rica created its Digital Nomad Visa with Law 10008 (the Law to Attract International Remote Workers and Service Providers, enacted August 11, 2021), later put into practice through an implementing regulation. Law 10208 (April 2022) clarified that the stay is granted for "up to" one year. In 2026, the visa is well-established and the processing pathway is clear. The Core Requirements

Costa Rica's Digital Nomad Visa (officially the Estancia para Trabajadores y Prestadores Remotos de Servicios de Carácter Internacional, a non-resident "stay" category) has three primary requirements, as of October 2026:

  • Income threshold: At least US$3,000/month in stable income over the previous year, received from outside Costa Rica (converted at the Central Bank's official selling rate). If your family group applies with you, the requirement is US$4,000/month in total. The income must come from paid remote work for employers or clients outside Costa Rica — the law bars holders from paid work in the Costa Rican market.
  • Remote work nature: You must be working remotely for a company or clients based outside Costa Rica.
  • Health insurance: A medical services policy with at least US$50,000 of coverage for illness-related medical expenses in Costa Rica, valid for the full authorized stay. It can be a local policy from an insurer authorized by Costa Rica's insurance superintendency (SUGESE) or an international policy with the same coverage. Each family member needs their own policy.

There is no minimum savings requirement and no language requirement. The visa does not require you to invest in Costa Rica or purchase property — it is purely income and remote work based.

Full Document Checklist

Documents Required for Application

  • Signed application form (filed online through Trámite Ya, or on paper at a DGME office)
  • Proof of the US$100 government payment (one separate transfer per applicant; the full $100 must arrive net of bank charges)
  • Copy of your passport photo page, plus the page with your Costa Rican entry stamp if you are already in the country
  • Consular or restricted visa, only if your nationality requires one
  • Proof of income for the previous year — bank statements with a sworn statement that you obtained them from the bank, or a certification from a public accountant or notary showing $3,000+/month ($4,000+ with family) from outside Costa Rica. Only the accountant/notary certification needs an apostille or consular legalization
  • Medical services policy with at least US$50,000 coverage for the full stay (one per person)
  • For a spouse: marriage certificate issued within the last six months (or civil-union document); for children: birth certificates (children under 25)
  • Official Spanish translation of any document in another language

Background checks and apostilles: As of October 2026, the official requirement list does not ask you to bring a home-country criminal record — DGME runs its own criminal and police record review and may take biometrics. The main apostille item is the accountant or notary income certification, if you use one; US applicants get apostilles from the Secretary of State of the state where the document was notarized. Family-relationship documents come from your home-country authorities.

The Application Process Step by Step

  1. Decide where to apply: from abroad or in Costa Rica

    You can apply online before you travel or after entering Costa Rica as a tourist. As of October 2026, citizens of "Group 1" countries (including the US, Canada, the UK and most of the EU) can be admitted visa-free for up to 180 days; the officer at entry sets the actual period. If you apply from inside the country, include your entry-stamp page.

  2. Gather your documents (start immediately)

    Collect 12 months of bank statements (or get the accountant/notary income certification and apostille it), buy a qualifying medical policy for each applicant, and request any marriage or birth certificates you need for family members.

  3. Get certified Spanish translations

    Documents in a language other than Spanish need an official translation, ideally by a translator registered with Costa Rica's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

  4. Hire a local immigration attorney (recommended, not mandatory)

    Not legally required — the official program page states that legal help is not needed. Some first-time applicants still hire a Costa Rican immigration attorney to review the file before submission and watch for requests for missing documents. Get quotes from several attorneys; fees vary.

  5. Submit to DGME and pay the US$100 application fee

    File online through the Trámite Ya platform (tramiteya.go.cr/dgme) or in person at a DGME central or regional office. Pay US$100 per applicant into the government account; factor in sending-bank charges and the US$15 fee Banco de Costa Rica charges on incoming international transfers.

  6. Wait for the decision

    Law 10008 gives DGME 15 calendar days to resolve a complete application. If DGME asks for missing items, the clock stops and you have up to 8 business days to respond. Actual timing can run longer than the statutory deadline, so keep track of your authorized tourist stay while you wait.

  7. Receive approval and get your immigration document

    After approval you have three months to process the document that certifies your legal stay. As of October 2026 this costs US$90, plus the government payment stated in your approval resolution and a one-time guarantee deposit for non-residents. Book the appointment through the Immigration call center (1311) or Banco de Costa Rica. This is a stay, not residency, but holders can open a Costa Rican bank account (subject to anti-money-laundering rules).

Tax Implications: What You Actually Owe

This is where nomads get confused. Clear breakdown:

Costa Rican taxes: Zero for the primary holder. Article 16 of Law 10008 gives beneficiaries a full exemption from Costa Rican income tax (the tax on profits under Law 7092) on the remote-work income covered by the visa, and the stay does not count as habitual residence for tax purposes. The exemption does not apply to accompanying family members. The law also exempts basic personal work equipment (computers, telecom devices) from import taxes, subject to conditions, and lets you drive on your valid home-country license during the stay.

Your home country taxes: This depends entirely on your nationality and tax residency situation. Key cases:

  • US citizens: The US taxes worldwide income regardless of where you live. However, the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) allows you to exclude up to $132,900 of foreign-earned income for tax year 2026 (indexed annually; $130,000 for 2025) if you meet the Physical Presence Test (330+ days outside the US in a 12-month period). The Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) can offset any residual US liability. Many US nomads in CR owe little or no net US tax, but you must file. Work with a cross-border CPA.
  • EU citizens: Tax rules vary by country. Most EU countries use residency-based taxation — if you're no longer a tax resident in your home country, you generally don't owe them tax. Requirements for exiting tax residency differ (Germany and France are strict; Estonia and Portugal have specific nomad schemes). Consult a tax advisor in your specific country.
  • Canadian citizens: Canada also taxes worldwide income for tax residents. "Factual residence" rules mean you can continue to owe Canadian tax even while abroad. Most nomads who leave Canada for 2+ years and sever ties (sell property, end provincial health coverage, close local accounts) can become non-resident for tax purposes. Seek Canadian tax advice before departing.

The FEIE Physical Presence Test in Practice

To claim the FEIE, you need 330+ days outside the US in any 12-month period (not calendar year). Nomads who spend most of the year in Costa Rica can usually meet this. Full days in Costa Rica or other foreign countries count; with 330 required days, you have roughly 35 days to spend in the US within the 12-month window. Keep a travel log — you claim the exclusion on Form 2555. With careful planning, US nomads whose foreign earned income is under the $132,900 limit (tax year 2026) can often owe little or no US income tax on that income, though self-employment tax still applies to freelancers.

Best Locations for Nomads in Costa Rica

San José (Escazú / Santa Ana)

The practical base for most new arrivals — best infrastructure, widely available fiber internet, most co-working spaces, easiest access to the DGME for visa processing. Not glamorous, but functional. Bolt access to the airport, hospitals, and shopping. As of October 2026, Numbeo's crowd-sourced average for a 1-bedroom apartment in central San José is about ₡429,000/month (about ₡346,000 outside the center); furnished units in Escazú and Santa Ana typically list above those averages. Convert at the current BCCR rate.

Tamarindo / Nosara (Guanacaste)

The beach nomad experience. Tamarindo has strong co-working infrastructure, good fiber internet in the town center, an international community, and direct flights to the US via Liberia airport (LIR). Nosara is more secluded — surf-focused, yoga-heavy, with higher prices than San José.

Santa Teresa

The most photogenic but least practical option. Beautiful, slow, excellent surf. Internet has improved significantly (fiber in parts of town) but power outages still occur. Roads require 4WD. Best for nomads who prioritize lifestyle over logistics and don't have video call-heavy work.

Puerto Viejo (Caribbean Coast)

Costa Rica's Caribbean side — reggae culture, Afro-Caribbean food, calmer sea, jungle. Generally cheaper than the Pacific beach towns. Slower pace, smaller nomad community. Internet is adequate in town but not reliable for heavy video work. Best for long-stay writers, creators, or location-independent workers without intensive connectivity needs.
