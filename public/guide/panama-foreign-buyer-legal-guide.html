Legal Guide — Panama

Can Foreigners Buy Property in Panama?
2026 Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy Updated October 17, 2026 10 min read

Yes. Foreigners can buy property in Panama with almost the same rights as citizens: no special permit, no government approval and no residency are needed to buy. The main exception is land within 10 km of a border. This guide covers the legal limits, closing costs, the transfer-tax rules for new builds (changed again in August 2026), property tax, and the residency routes tied to property. It also covers the new Qualified Investor decree that took effect on September 16, 2026.

Updated October 17, 2026. Tax and visa rules change often in Panama; confirm the rule that applies on your closing date with a licensed Panamanian attorney.

Ownership Rights: What Foreigners Can (and Cannot) Buy

You can own titled property outright in your own name, through a Panamanian corporation or through a private interest foundation. There is no foreign-buyer approval board. The Constitution sets a few limits:

  • Border zone (Constitution, Art. 291): no foreign person, and no Panamanian company with any foreign capital, may own land less than 10 kilometers from a national border. This mainly matters for rural land in Chiriquí and Bocas del Toro (near Costa Rica) and Darién (near Colombia). Confirm the distance before you make an offer.
  • Islands: island land is generally available only through a government concession, not outright ownership, according to the Icaza, González-Ruiz & Alemán real estate guide. Check the title type carefully on any island purchase.
  • Indigenous comarcas (Constitution, Art. 127): land in the indigenous territories is held collectively. This limit applies to everyone, Panamanians included.
Key Takeaway

Outside border land, islands and comarcas, a foreigner can buy titled property in Panama much as a citizen can. You need a passport, an attorney and a notary, not a visa.

Titled Property vs. Rights of Possession

Not all land in Panama carries the same legal protections. Know which kind you are buying before you sign.

Titled Property (Finca)

A titled property is registered in the Public Registry (Registro Público) with its own folio number. Titled property is the easiest to finance, insure and resell, and it is what residency programs ask for. Always have your attorney run a title search to check for liens, mortgages and ownership disputes.

Rights of Possession (Derecho Posesorio)

Some rural and coastal land is held only as derecho posesorio: a claim to occupy and use land that has not been titled. It can sometimes be converted to title through an adjudication process, but that takes time and is not guaranteed. Possession rights carry much more risk for a foreign buyer and are generally not accepted for property-based residency. Avoid them unless you have an experienced local attorney.

The Buying Process: Step by Step

Global Property Guide puts the timeline at about 1–3 months from accepted offer to registered title. The whole process runs between private parties.

  1. Hire a Panamanian real estate attorney. Using one is customary and strongly advised. Your attorney runs the title search, drafts the contracts and coordinates the closing. Global Property Guide lists legal fees at about 2% of the price.

  2. Sign a promise-to-buy agreement (promesa de compraventa). It fixes the price, terms and closing date. The deposit is negotiated. Make sure the contract says what happens to it if the deal falls through.

  3. Do due diligence. Your attorney checks the Public Registry for liens, confirms property tax is paid, confirms the seller's legal standing and, for rural land, looks for possession claims and border distance.

  4. Sign the public deed (escritura pública). The transfer deed is executed before a Panamanian notary. Buyer and seller, or their legal representatives, sign it.

  5. Register the transfer at the Public Registry. Registration makes the transfer effective against third parties. Registry fees are paid at this stage.

Important

Pay through a regulated channel such as your attorney's or a bank's escrow, never in cash to a seller. Panama has strict anti-money-laundering rules, and banks and lawyers will ask you to document the source of your funds. Allow extra time for bank compliance checks on large international transfers.

Closing Costs Breakdown

Global Property Guide estimates buyer-side costs at about 2.1% of the price. The 2% transfer tax is legally the seller's, and a private agreement cannot transfer it to the buyer (La Prensa), though sellers may build it into the price. Round-trip costs for a buy and a later sale run about 7–9% (GPG: 7.1–9.1%). Mortgage bank fees come on top.

New-build transfer tax: what applies in 2026

For decades the first sale of a new home was exempt from the 2% transfer tax. Ley 468 (April 24, 2025) repealed that exemption; Ley 472 (June 2025) restored it until December 31, 2025; from January 1, 2026 there was no exemption. Then Ley 546 of August 31, 2026 brought back partial relief on new homes: the first US$120,000 of the taxable value is exempt (the Ministry of Economy describes it as applying to the purchase of a first new home, so confirm eligibility). For homes priced up to US$200,000, only the amount above US$120,000 is taxed, at stepped rates from 0.5% to 1.8%. The sale must close within two years of the occupancy permit (30 months for older permits, as a transition). Homes priced above US$200,000 get no relief.

Annual Property Taxes

Panama taxes real property on its registered value using progressive rates set by Ley 66 of 2017 and in force since January 1, 2019. The rates depend on whether the property is registered as your primary residence.

Primary Residence (Patrimonio Familiar Tributario / Vivienda Principal)

If you register the property as your primary residence with the DGI (Panama's tax authority), the first US$120,000 of registered value is exempt:

New construction: the old 20-, 10- and 5-year exemptions on improvements (Ley 28 of 2012) apply only to buildings whose construction permit was issued before January 1, 2019. Ley 66 of 2017 replaced them with a narrower rule: a first home purchase registered as your primary residence, with a value between US$120,000 and US$300,000, is exempt for 3 years from the occupancy permit or registry inscription, whichever comes first. Ley 468 changed the transfer tax, not property tax. Ask your attorney to confirm what applies to your unit.

Example: an investment condo with a registered value of US$200,000 pays 0.6% on US$170,000 (the part above US$30,000), or about US$1,020 a year. Registered as a primary residence, the same unit pays 0.5% on US$80,000, or about US$400.

Residency Pathways Through Real Estate

Three common residency routes can be tied to buying property. Requirements are set by executive decree and change; your attorney should confirm the current rule before you buy.

Friendly NationsUS$200,000 in titled real estate2-year provisional, then permanent

PensionadoUS$750/month pension plus property over US$100,000 (or US$1,000/month pension alone)Permanent

1. Friendly Nations Visa

Citizens of countries on Panama's Friendly Nations list (it includes Canada, the UK and many European and Latin American countries, and Ecuador was added in August 2026) can apply by showing economic ties. Check that your nationality is on the current list. Under Executive Decrees 197 and 226 of 2021, one qualifying option is:

  • Titled real estate worth at least US$200,000 (a US$200,000 fixed deposit held for 3 years is an alternative)
  • A 2-year provisional residency, after which you can apply for permanent residency
  • If you plan to finance the purchase, ask counsel whether a mortgaged property counts at full value

2. Qualified Investor Visa

Executive Decree No. 17 of September 8, 2026 (Gaceta Oficial No. 30613) replaced the 2020 decree and its extensions. It has been in force since September 16, 2026. The decree sets no October 15, 2026 deadline; an earlier version of this guide wrongly said the threshold rose on that date. The current real-estate minimums are:

  • US$300,000 for first-sale (new) property, including an off-plan purchase promise
  • US$500,000 for resale (secondary-market) property
  • Other options: US$500,000 in securities, a US$500,000 fixed deposit at Banco Nacional or Caja de Ahorros, or US$750,000 at a private bank
  • Grants permanent residency directly. The investment must be kept for 5 years.
  • Transition: investments or binding contracts made before September 16, 2026 can use the old rules if the application is filed within 6 months

3. Pensionado (Retiree) Visa

  • Option A: a lifetime pension of at least US$1,000/month
  • Option B: a US$750/month pension plus property worth more than US$100,000 held in your personal name
  • Grants permanent residency
  • Ley 6 of 1987 (as amended) gives retirees discounts, including 15% on hospital and clinic bills, 20% on doctor visits, 25% on airfares, 25% on electricity (up to 600 kWh), and up to 50% on hotels and entertainment

Practical Checklist for Foreign Buyers

  • ✓  Hire a bilingual Panamanian real estate attorney before making any offer
  • ✓  Confirm the property is titled (finca), not derecho posesorio or an island concession
  • ✓  Run a full title search through the Public Registry
  • ✓  Verify property tax is paid and there are no outstanding liens
  • ✓  For rural land: confirm it is more than 10 km from any border
  • ✓  Pay through escrow or your attorney, never in cash to the seller
  • ✓  Budget about 2–3% for buyer-side closing costs; confirm the seller has paid the 2% transfer tax
  • ✓  Buying new? Ask whether the Ley 546 transfer-tax relief applies
  • ✓  Using the property for residency: confirm whether it is first-sale or resale (US$300,000 vs US$500,000 for Qualified Investor)
Bottom Line

Panama remains one of the most straightforward places in Latin America for a foreigner to buy property. It uses the US dollar, has a public title registry and offers several property-linked residency routes. The rules move, though: 2025–2026 brought three changes to the new-build transfer tax and a new investor-visa decree. Work with a licensed Panamanian attorney, stick to titled property, and get your costs and visa threshold confirmed in writing.

FAQ: Buying Property in Panama as a Foreigner

Can a foreigner own property in Panama?

Yes. Foreigners can own titled property in their own name, or through a corporation or foundation, with almost the same rights as citizens and no residency requirement. The Constitution (Art. 291) bars foreigners from owning land within 10 km of a border. Island land is generally available only by government concession.

What is the minimum real estate investment for Panama's Qualified Investor visa?

Under Executive Decree No. 17 of 2026, in force since September 16, 2026, the minimum is US$300,000 for first-sale (new) property and US$500,000 for resale property. Investments made before that date can follow the old rules if the application is filed within 6 months.

Do new homes in Panama still pay the 2% transfer tax?

Partly. Under Ley 546 of August 31, 2026, a new home is exempt on its first US$120,000 of value. Homes up to US$200,000 pay reduced rates of 0.5–1.8% on the excess, if the sale closes within two years of the occupancy permit. Homes above US$200,000 get no relief.
