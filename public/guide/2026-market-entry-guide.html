Section 01

Executive Summary — The Four Markets in 2026

Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Argentina and Chile are four very different propositions for a foreign buyer. Costa Rica and Chile let foreigners buy titled property on the same terms as citizens under predictable rules. Argentina offers dollar-priced Buenos Aires apartments in an economy that is still stabilizing. Nicaragua is cheap because its government has been seizing property.

This guide covers these 4 of the 11 Latin American markets International RE tracks. For a side-by-side comparison of 17 cities in 10 countries, including Mexico, Panama, Colombia and Uruguay, see our free Expat Starter Kit. Every figure below was checked on October 19, 2026 against the sources listed at the end of the guide.

The 2026 Picture

  • Prices: Buenos Aires apartments average US$2,484/m² (Zonaprop, September 2026), 11.3% below the index's all-time high. New apartments in San José, Costa Rica averaged about $2,949/m² in Q3 2026, up 10.7% in a year (Global Property Guide). New apartments in the Santiago region averaged 83 UF/m², about $3,500, up 5.4% in a year in UF terms (TOCTOC). There is no official price index for Nicaragua.
  • Rental yields (gross): about 7.6% on average in Costa Rica (Q2 2026), 5.70% across Buenos Aires (September 2026) and 4.80% in Chile (Q3 2026). These are before vacancy, management, maintenance and tax; Global Property Guide puts Chilean net yields 1.5–2 points lower.
  • Currency: property is usually quoted in US dollars in Costa Rica, Nicaragua and Argentina, and in UF (an inflation-indexed unit) in Chile. Nicaragua is not dollarized: its córdoba has been fixed at 36.6243 per dollar since January 2024. Argentina's official and parallel dollar rates were about 1% apart in October 2026, and annual inflation was 33.4% in September 2026.
  • Foreign ownership: Costa Rica's constitution and Chile's Civil Code give foreigners the same property rights as citizens, with exceptions for Costa Rica's beachfront Maritime Zone and, in Chile, border zones for nationals of neighbouring countries. Argentina allows foreigners to buy urban property freely, but the Supreme Court restored limits on foreign ownership of rural land in October 2026. Nicaragua has no formal restriction outside border and security areas, but the US State Department reports seizures of foreign-owned property.
  • Mortgages: plan to pay cash or finance at home. Chilean banks such as Bci and Banco Consorcio lend only to citizens or foreigners with permanent residency, and in Buenos Aires only 1,059 of 6,055 property deeds in August 2026 involved a mortgage.
  • Growth: the IMF's October 2026 outlook expects Chile's economy to grow just 0.6% in 2026 and Argentina's 2.1%.

◆ How to Read the Numbers

Price data come from different sources and measure different things: new-build averages (Global Property Guide, TOCTOC) usually sit above asking-price averages for older stock (Zonaprop). Yields are gross. Taxes change often in all four countries, and every figure here should be confirmed with your own lawyer and tax adviser before you sign. We do not forecast appreciation.

How to Use This Guide

Each market section covers the price and yield data that exist from a named source, the legal process for foreign buyers, closing costs and taxes, and the main risks. The guide closes with a comparison table, a universal buying checklist, three common questions and the full source list.

Section 02

🇨🇷 Costa Rica

The Most Established Foreign-Buyer Market — Equal Rights, Dollar Pricing, Rising Central Valley Prices

$2,949 San José new-build $/m²
7.6% Avg gross yield
Low Legal Risk

Costa Rica is the most established market for foreign buyers of the four. Article 19 of its constitution gives foreigners the same rights as citizens, the US State Department describes it as the region's oldest continuous democracy, and most property is priced in US dollars. The trade-off is price: new apartments in San José averaged about $2,949/m² in Q3 2026.

Market Overview

Global Property Guide's data for new apartments in the Central Valley (based on Encuentra24 listings) show prices rising over the past year: up 10.7% in San José, 10.5% in Curridabat, 7.8% in Escazú and 7.4% in Santa Ana to Q3 2026. Its average gross rental yield for Costa Rica was 7.63% in Q2 2026, ranging from 6.29% in Escazú to 8.69% in Heredia.

There is no comparable published index for the beach markets. Asking prices in Guanacaste and on the South Pacific coast vary widely by project and distance from the beach, and vacation-rental income is seasonal, so ask any seller for registered comparable sales and at least two years of actual booking and expense records.

Where Foreign Buyers Look

Guanacaste (Tamarindo, Playas del Coco, Flamingo, Nosara, the Papagayo Peninsula) is the most established tourism and vacation-rental area, served by Liberia's international airport (LIR).

The South Pacific (Dominical, Uvita, Ojochal) is quieter, with more limited infrastructure the further south you go. Check road access and water supply before anything else.

The Central Valley (Escazú, Santa Ana, Curridabat, San José) suits buyers who want long-term tenants rather than vacation guests, and is the only part of the country with regularly published price and yield data.

Legal Process for Foreigners

Foreigners can own titled property in their own name or through a Costa Rican company, without residency or a local partner. If you use a company, you take on annual corporate tax and beneficial-ownership filings.

  1. Hire your own attorney — independent of the seller and the agent. Costa Rican notaries are lawyers and can register title changes, so this matters.
  2. Title search and due diligence — your attorney checks the National Registry for liens, mortgages and annotations, the registered survey (plano catastrado), municipal taxes and, near the coast, Maritime Zone status. Get a water availability letter before buying land.
  3. Option or purchase agreement — sets the price, deposit, conditions and closing date. Put deposits and purchase funds through an escrow agent registered with SUGEF, the financial regulator; escrow typically costs about 0.25% or a flat fee near $1,000.
  4. Closing before a notary — the notary drafts the deed and registers the transfer with the National Registry.
  5. Closing costs — a 1.5% transfer tax on the higher of the sale price or the registered fiscal value, roughly 0.5–0.8% in stamps and registration fees, and notary fees of generally 1–2% plus VAT. The total is roughly 3.5–5% of the price, customarily split between buyer and seller, though the split is negotiable.

Timeline

The registration steps themselves are quick: in the World Bank's last benchmark (data from May 2019), transferring a clean, already-registered property in San José took five procedures and 11 days. Your real timeline depends on negotiation, due diligence, financing and, for coastal property, concession approvals.

⚠ Maritime Zone Law — Critical for Beach Properties

Under Law 6043, the 200 metres inland from the ordinary high-tide line belong to the state. The first 50 metres are a public zone that no one can own or build on. The remaining 150 metres are a restricted zone that can only be held under a municipal concession, which is a lease, not freehold title. Article 47 bars concessions to foreigners who have not resided in Costa Rica for at least five years, to companies domiciled abroad or formed in Costa Rica by foreigners, and to companies more than 50% foreign-owned. Be very wary of structures offered to get around this rule.

Tax Implications

  • Annual property tax: 0.25% of the registered value.
  • Luxury home tax (Impuesto Solidario): for 2026, homes whose construction value exceeds ₡143 million pay a progressive tax of 0.25% to 0.55% charged on the property's total fiscal value (land and construction), not just the part above the threshold.
  • Rental income tax: 15% on 85% of gross rent (a standard 15% deduction), an effective 12.75% of gross rent, for residents and non-residents.
  • Capital gains tax: 15% of the gain. For property acquired before July 1, 2019, you can instead pay 2.25% of the sale price on the first sale. The sale of your habitual home is exempt, and when the seller is a non-resident the buyer withholds 2.5% of the price.
  • No inheritance or gift tax in Costa Rica.

For the full detail, including residency options, see our Costa Rica buying costs and taxes guide.

Section 03

🇳🇮 Nicaragua

Cheap for a Reason — Property Seizures, Unreliable Title and No Reliable Price Data

No index Price data
Level 3 US travel advisory
High Legal Risk

Nicaragua is one of Central America's cheapest property markets, and in 2026 its riskiest. The US State Department reports that the government has seized private and foreign-owned property, the constitution was rewritten in 2025, and in August 2026 the government took nine homes owned by US retirees on the Pacific coast. Prices are low for a reason; read our sourced 2026 risk briefing on buying in Granada before anything below.

Market Overview

We found no official house-price index for Nicaragua, and Global Property Guide no longer publishes data for it, so this guide does not print per-square-metre prices or rental yields for Nicaragua. Any figure you are quoted is an asking price.

The tourism that supports rental demand in San Juan del Sur and Granada has shrunk: Infobae reported in September 2026 that Nicaragua lost more than 120,000 visitors between 2023 and 2025 and that air arrivals of tourists fell 40%. Prices are commonly quoted in US dollars, but the country is not dollarized; the córdoba has been fixed at 36.6243 per dollar since January 1, 2024.

The confiscation risk is current, not historical. According to La Prensa, the government took nine luxury homes owned by US retirees in Tola, Rivas, in August 2026 and transferred them to the social security institute (INSS); eight owners got their keys back in October. The State Department also reports that from 2020 to April 2026 the government transferred 110 confiscated properties to the INSS.

Where Foreigners Have Bought

Foreign buyers have concentrated in San Juan del Sur and the nearby Pacific beaches, colonial Granada and, for long-term living, Managua. The US Embassy advises "extreme caution" before buying property in Nicaragua, especially coastal property. See our guides to Granada and San Juan del Sur.

Legal Process for Foreigners

The US State Department's 2026 Investment Climate Statement reports no specific restrictions on foreign or non-resident buyers apart from border and national-security areas, so a foreigner can still buy in their own name. But the same report says the government has seized foreign-owned property, and a sweeping constitutional rewrite in January 2025 placed the courts under the executive. A 2025 law (Law 1258) put all land within 15 km of the land borders under state and military control (sources). If you still proceed, the process has these steps:

  1. Hire an attorney experienced with foreign transactions — independent of the seller and agent, and early in the process, as the State Department recommends.
  2. Title verification — critically important. The US Embassy estimates about 28,000 properties were expropriated between 1979 and 1990, many still disputed, and the State Department warns of a "cottage industry" in false titles and that squatters can obtain ownership of unoccupied property, particularly with government backing. Your attorney should trace the chain of title back through that period.
  3. Purchase agreement and deposit — keep the deposit in a trust or escrow arrangement, not with the seller.
  4. Closing via Notary — a public notary executes the deed, which is then registered at the property registry, now under the Attorney General's office.
  5. Closing costs — the transfer is subject to an income-tax withholding of 1% to 7% of the value, rising with price (Ley 987); agree in writing who pays it. Notary, registry and legal fees come on top, so ask your lawyer for a written estimate.

⚠ Risks to Understand Before Buying

  • Title risk: conflicting land title claims are, in the State Department's words, abundant, and judicial appeals are slow. The State Department says it is not possible to determine what share of land lacks clear title.
  • Confiscation and political risk: The US State Department reports seizures of foreign-owned assets, rates Nicaragua Level 3, "Reconsider travel", and reports that the property registry now sits under the Attorney General's office. Only commit money you could afford to lose.
  • Liquidity risk: with fewer visitors and buyers, selling can take a long time, especially in a hurry.
  • Infrastructure gap: power, water and road quality outside the main towns and tourist zones is inconsistent.

Tax Implications

  • Annual property tax (IBI): 1% of 80% of the cadastral value (an effective 0.8%).
  • Rental income tax: rental income is a capital income taxed at 15% for residents and non-residents on net rent after a fixed 20% deduction, about 12% of gross rent (Ley 987, arts. 80 and 87).
  • Tax on sale: a sale of registered property is taxed through a definitive withholding of 1% to 7% of the property's value on a sliding scale (Ley 987, art. 87), rather than a separate tax on the gain.
  • Tourism incentives: the old tourism incentive law (Ley 306) was repealed by the 2024 General Tourism Law (Ley 1210), which has its own incentive regime for qualifying tourism developments. It is aimed at tourism projects; check eligibility with a lawyer.

Section 04

🇦🇷 Argentina

Dollar-Priced Buenos Aires — 11% Below Its Peak, in an Economy Still Stabilizing

$2,484 BA Avg $/m²
5.7% BA gross yield
Medium Legal Risk

Buenos Aires apartments are listed, sold and paid for in US dollars. Zonaprop's September 2026 index puts the city average at US$2,484/m², up 1.4% so far in 2026 and still 11.3% below the index's all-time high, with a city-wide gross rental yield of 5.70%. The opportunity is real, but so is the macro risk, and the old "blue dollar" discount has gone.

Market Overview

Prices have been broadly flat in dollar terms: up 1.4% in the first nine months of 2026, according to Zonaprop. Rents, which are set in pesos, rose 28.7% in the 12 months to September 2026, against annual inflation of 33.4% in September (INDEC). Sales volumes are modest: the Buenos Aires notaries' association recorded 6,055 deeds in August 2026, 4.9% fewer than a year earlier.

President Milei's government lifted most currency controls for individuals in April 2025, and the official and parallel ("blue") dollar rates were about 1% apart in mid-October 2026 (ARS 1,535 and 1,545). That convergence makes transactions simpler, but it also means foreigners no longer get a large discount by changing money at the parallel rate. Rules have changed repeatedly: in September 2025 the government reinstated a 90-day restriction stopping individuals who buy official dollars from also buying dollar financial assets.

Dollar Pricing: What It Does and Doesn't Do

Because apartments are priced in dollars, a peso devaluation does not automatically cut what you pay or what you can sell for. But rents are paid in pesos, so your dollar yield depends on whether rents keep pace with the exchange rate, and running costs such as expensas (building fees) and the city's ABL property levy are also set in pesos. Barrios popular with foreigners are among the lowest-yielding: 4.6% in Palermo and 3.2% in Puerto Madero, against 5.70% for the city.

Rentals and the Short-Term Market

Short-term rentals are legal in Buenos Aires, but every unit must be registered, free of charge, in the city's tourist rental register under Law 6,255. Tax treatment differs by rental type: from 2026, income from long-term rentals of homes used as the tenant's permanent residence is exempt from national income tax (Global Property Guide says this also applies to non-resident individuals; confirm with an Argentine accountant), while short-term rental income of a non-resident is generally taxed at 35% on a presumed 60% of gross rent, about 21% of gross. Ask local managers for actual occupancy and net income records rather than relying on listing-site estimates.

Legal Process for Foreigners

Foreigners can buy urban property in Argentina in their own name without residency or a local partner. Rural land is different: on October 9, 2026, the Supreme Court provisionally suspended the decree that had repealed the Rural Land Law, restoring caps on foreign ownership of rural land.

  1. Get a tax ID (CUIT) — since March 2, 2026, ARCA (the former AFIP) issues non-resident individuals a CUIT instead of the old CDI, requested through a representative in Argentina.
  2. Plan how the money arrives — most deals are settled in dollars. Agree with your escribano how funds will be transferred and documented before you sign. Our guide to transferring money abroad to buy property covers comparing providers and avoiding wire fraud.
  3. Hire an escribano (notary) and, ideally, your own lawyer — the escribano runs title and debt checks and executes the deed.
  4. Sign the boleto de compraventa — the purchase contract, usually with a negotiated deposit.
  5. Closing (escritura) — the escribano formalizes the transfer and registers it with the Property Registry.
  6. Closing costs — in the City of Buenos Aires, stamp duty is 2.7% of the price (3.5% above ARS 226.1 million; 0% for a sole family home up to that amount), plus escribano fees, agent commission, certificates and registration. Ámbito puts total buyer costs at about 5–8% in the city and 8–12% in Buenos Aires Province. The old 1.5% property transfer tax (ITI) was repealed in July 2024.

◆ Argentina Investment Profile Summary

Best for: buyers paying cash in dollars who want a city apartment in Buenos Aires and can live with macroeconomic volatility.

Not ideal for: buyers who need a mortgage, who may need to sell quickly, or who want predictable rules.

Watch carefully: currency and tax rules have changed often, including in 2025 and 2026. Engage a tax adviser in both your home country and Argentina before committing.

Tax Implications

  • ABL (City of Buenos Aires property levy): based on the property's fiscal valuation; ask the seller for recent bills.
  • Bienes Personales (personal assets tax): for owners living abroad, 0.5% of the value of Argentine assets each year, paid in full and final settlement through a local representative.
  • Rental income tax: from 2026, long-term residential rentals are exempt from national income tax for residents and non-residents; short-term rentals by non-residents are taxed at about 21% of gross. Local gross-income tax may also apply.
  • Capital gains: from January 1, 2026, gains on the sale of Argentine property by individuals, resident or living abroad, are exempt from income tax (Decreto 406/2026). Before 2026, a 15% tax applied to property acquired from 2018. The rules are new, so confirm your case with an accountant.

Section 05

🇨🇱 Chile

Strong Institutions and UF-Indexed Prices — but Slow Growth and No Mortgages for Non-Residents

≈$3,500 Santiago new apts $/m²
5.0% Santiago gross yield
Low Legal Risk

Chile is the region's most institutionally predictable market. Article 57 of the Civil Code gives foreigners the same civil rights as Chileans, the legal process is standardized, and prices are set in UF, an inflation-indexed unit published daily by the tax authority (CLP 41,183.94 on October 19, 2026). The downsides are slow growth, modest yields and the fact that banks lend only to residents.

Market Overview

New apartments in the Santiago region averaged 83 UF/m² (about $3,500) in the three months to July 2026, 5.4% more than a year earlier, according to TOCTOC data reported by CNN Chile. The eastern communes are the most expensive: Vitacura 117, Las Condes 114 and Providencia 103 UF/m². Measured in dollars by Global Property Guide (using Chilean Chamber of Construction data), Santiago new apartments averaged $2,810/m² in Q3 2026, down 16.1% in a year; the two sources measure different things, and the UF–dollar rate moves.

Supply is mixed. The stock of new apartments for sale in the Santiago region fell 18.2% in a year to 49,834 units at the end of July 2026, but the Chilean Chamber of Construction says about 100,000 homes remain unsold nationally, which has slowed new projects. The IMF expects the economy to grow just 0.6% in 2026. A reconstruction law promulgated in October 2026 is expected, once published in the Diario Oficial, to exempt the first sale of finished new homes from VAT for one year (from about December 1, 2026), which may affect developer pricing.

Santiago

Las Condes, Vitacura and Lo Barnechea are the city's wealthiest communes and its most expensive. Providencia and Ñuñoa are denser, walkable and well served by the metro, and attract younger professionals and long-term renters. Global Property Guide's Santiago yields average 5.01% gross; in its central-Santiago sample, one-bedroom apartments cost about $77,000–$91,000. See our Santiago neighborhood guide for commune-by-commune detail.

Valparaíso & the Coast

The Historic Quarter of Valparaíso is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and its hillside cerros draw cultural tourism and lifestyle buyers. Viña del Mar, next door, is a conventional beach resort with demand from Santiago weekenders. Gross yields on the coast are lower than in Santiago: 4.19% in Valparaíso and 3.81% in Viña del Mar.

The Lake District

The Lake District around Pucón and Puerto Varas, with its volcanoes and lakes, is Chile's main lifestyle destination for foreign buyers, built around outdoor tourism. We found no current published price or yield index for it, so treat asking prices and rental projections with care, ask for actual booking records, and compare with the analysis in our Santiago vs the Lake District guide.

Legal Process for Foreigners

Chile's property process is transparent and standardized. Foreigners can buy freely, except that nationals of neighbouring countries cannot acquire property in designated border zones without special authorization.

  1. Obtain a RUT (Rol Único Tributario) — Chile's tax ID, required for non-residents buying property. Non-residents who earn income in Chile must appoint a representative in the country.
  2. Hire a Chilean attorney — legal fees of about 1% of the price, according to Global Property Guide.
  3. Sign a promesa de compraventa — a notarized promise of sale with a deposit. For off-plan purchases, make sure your deposit is guaranteed.
  4. Mortgage — generally not available to non-residents. Bci lends to Chileans and to foreigners with permanent residency; Banco Consorcio requires at least two years of permanent residency and lends up to 80%. Mortgages are set in UF; the average rate was 3.97% in June 2026 (Banco Central de Chile).
  5. Closing before a notary — both parties sign the deed, and ownership passes when it is registered at the Conservador de Bienes Raíces (property registry).
  6. Closing costs — notary fees of about 0.3–0.4%, legal fees of about 1%, and a buyer's agent fee of 1–2% if one is used: about 2.3–3.4% in total (Global Property Guide).

✓ Chile Advantage — Low, Predictable Closing Costs

At about 2.3–3.4% of the price, Chile's buyer closing costs are among the lowest of the markets in this guide (Costa Rica's 3.5–5% total is usually split between buyer and seller, so compare like with like), and the process is the most standardized. Buyers who prioritize legal certainty often choose Chile despite higher prices and lower yields.

Tax Implications

  • Annual property tax (Contribuciones): for homes, 0.893% of the tax appraisal above an exempt amount of CLP 61,711,570, and 1.042% on the part of the appraisal above CLP 220,398,431 (second half of 2026).
  • Rental income tax: non-residents pay the 35% Additional Tax on net rental income, with a credit for first-category tax. Global Property Guide estimates the effective burden at about 24.5% of gross rent, assuming 30% deductible expenses.
  • Capital gains tax: for non-residents, the gain is subject to the 35% Additional Tax. The lifetime exemption of 8,000 UF of gain and the optional 10% single tax apply only to individuals domiciled or resident in Chile.
  • VAT on new homes: sales of new homes by developers are subject to VAT, which is included in the asking price. The reconstruction law promulgated in October 2026 is expected, once published, to exempt the first sale of finished new homes for one year from about December 1, 2026; check whether a given unit qualifies.

Section 07

Step-by-Step Buying Checklist

This checklist applies across all four markets in this guide. Print it out or save it as a PDF alongside this guide. Use it to track your progress from first inquiry to holding title.

Phase 1 — Research & Market Selection

  • Define your investment objective Rental income, personal use, retirement or a mix? Each market serves different goals. Review the comparison table above.
  • Set a realistic budget including all costs Buyer closing costs range from about 2.3–3.4% in Chile to about 5–8% in the City of Buenos Aires (in Costa Rica, 3.5–5% in total, usually split with the seller). Add furnishing, utility connections and a reserve for the first year's taxes and fees. Assume you will pay cash.
  • Research target areas online before visiting Use published indices (Zonaprop for Buenos Aires, Global Property Guide, TOCTOC for Santiago), local listing portals and expat groups, and remember that listings show asking prices, not sale prices.
  • Plan a scouting trip of at least 2 weeks Rent in your target area before buying. Seasonal variation in tourism areas means different months feel dramatically different.

Phase 2 — Legal and Financial Preparation

  • Hire a qualified local attorney before making any offer Never rely only on an attorney recommended by the seller or agent. Seek independent referrals from expat communities or your embassy.
  • Consult a tax advisor in your home country US citizens: foreign real estate held directly is not reported on the FBAR or Form 8938, but foreign bank accounts above $10,000 in aggregate are (FBAR), and a foreign company may trigger Form 5471. Other nationalities have their own rules.
  • Obtain the local tax identification number CUIT (Argentina, which replaced the CDI for individuals in March 2026), RUT (Chile). In Costa Rica and Nicaragua, ask your attorney what identification and tax registration the notary and registry need.
  • Establish how funds will be transferred internationally Wire transfers are standard. Confirm compliance requirements with both your sending bank and the receiving country's rules. In Costa Rica use a SUGEF-registered escrow agent; in Argentina agree the settlement method with your escribano.
  • Determine ownership structure (individual vs. corporate entity) A local company can help with liability or estate planning but brings annual taxes and filings. Buying an existing company that owns the property means inheriting its history. Decide with your attorney and home-country tax adviser.

Phase 3 — Due Diligence

  • Order a full title search through your attorney Verify chain of title, confirm no liens or encumbrances, check for boundary disputes. In Nicaragua, trace title back through the 1979–1990 expropriation period. In Costa Rica, verify Maritime Zone status for coastal properties.
  • Confirm legal boundaries with a registered surveyor Cadastral maps and actual physical boundaries sometimes diverge. A certified survey is particularly important for land purchases and rural properties.
  • Conduct a physical inspection by a licensed engineer or inspector Check for water damage, mold, foundation issues and electrical and plumbing condition. In Chile, ask how the building complies with the seismic code. Get a written quote for the inspection in advance.
  • Verify utilities (water, power, internet) connectivity and cost Especially important in rural Nicaragua, Costa Rica's South Pacific, and remote Lake District properties in Chile. In Costa Rica, get a water availability letter before buying land.
  • Research HOA/condo fees if purchasing in a managed development Building and community fees vary widely; in Buenos Aires, expensas are set in pesos and rise with inflation. Request 12 months of financial statements from the administrator.

Phase 4 — Purchase and Closing

  • Negotiate and sign purchase agreement with your attorney present Ensure contingencies are included: clear title and satisfactory inspection. Hold the deposit in escrow or trust, not with the seller.
  • Arrange international wire transfer of closing funds Allow several business days for international transfers to clear. Notify your bank in advance to avoid compliance delays on large transfers.
  • Attend closing or provide a power of attorney If you cannot be present, your attorney can act under a properly executed power of attorney. Ask in advance whether it must be notarized and apostilled in your home country.
  • Confirm property registration and obtain title documents Verify your name (or entity name) appears correctly in the property registry. Request certified copies of the recorded deed. In Costa Rica, sign up for the Registry's title-alert service.

Phase 5 — Post-Purchase

  • Obtain property insurance Structure, contents (for rentals) and liability cover. Compare local insurers with international ones and read the exclusions, especially for earthquakes and storms.
  • Set up local bank account for operating expenses Simplifies payment of property taxes, utility bills, HOA fees, and property manager invoices. Required for tax compliance in some jurisdictions.
  • Engage a reputable property management company (for rentals) Interview at least 3 candidates. Review their current portfolio occupancy, maintenance response protocols and owner reporting, and get their fees in writing. Register short-term rentals where required (for example, Buenos Aires).
  • Register for local property tax and comply with annual filings Your local attorney can handle initial registration. Annual tax payments and rental income declarations (where required) should be calendared.
  • Update your home country tax filings Report rental income and foreign tax paid, plus any foreign accounts (FBAR, Form 8938) and companies (Form 5471 for US persons). Review your obligations with a qualified tax professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners get a mortgage to buy property in Latin America?

Rarely as non-residents. In Chile, banks such as Bci and Banco Consorcio lend only to Chileans or foreigners with permanent residency, and Consorcio requires at least two years of it. In Buenos Aires, only 1,059 of 6,055 property deeds in August 2026 involved a mortgage of any kind. In Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Argentina and Chile alike, plan to pay cash or borrow in your home country.

What are closing costs for foreign buyers in Costa Rica, Argentina, Chile and Nicaragua?

About 2.3–3.4% of the price for the buyer in Chile (Global Property Guide); roughly 3.5–5% in total in Costa Rica, customarily split between buyer and seller (Tico Times); and about 5–8% for the buyer in the City of Buenos Aires (Ámbito). In Nicaragua, every transfer is subject to an income-tax withholding of 1–7% of the value (Ley 987), plus notary, registry and legal fees.

Is it safe for a foreigner to buy property in Nicaragua in 2026?

It carries the highest risk of the four markets in this guide. The US State Department reports that the government has seized foreign-owned property, moved the property registry under the Attorney General's office and, in 2025, put all land within 15 km of the land borders under state and military control. In August 2026 it took nine homes owned by US retirees in Tola; eight were returned in October. The State Department rates Nicaragua Level 3, "Reconsider travel". Only commit money you could afford to lose.
