Property Tax in Uruguay for Foreigners 2026

By Charles Brossy June 3, 2026 3 min read

Uruguay is one of the most tax-friendly destinations for foreign property buyers in Latin America. Foreigners pay exactly the same property tax rates as Uruguayan citizens — no surcharge, no nationality restrictions. The annual Contribución Inmobiliaria runs 0.25%–0.50% of fiscal value, and a $200,000 Montevideo apartment typically costs just $400–$1,000 per year to hold.

The Contribución Inmobiliaria: How It Works

Uruguay's main annual property tax is the Contribución Inmobiliaria, administered by each department (similar to a province or county). In Montevideo, the rate for urban residential properties is 0.25%–0.50% of valor fiscal — the government's assessed value.

The key detail: valor fiscal is set by the Dirección Nacional de Catastro and is almost always well below market price. In Pocitos and Punta Carretas — Montevideo's most sought-after neighborhoods — fiscal values typically run 30–50% below market. That gap keeps your real annual bill far lower than the headline rate suggests.

On top of the Contribución, there is a small Contribución al Fondo de Educación Primaria (primary education levy) of roughly 0.10% of fiscal value. Combined, most Montevideo homeowners pay an all-in rate under 0.60% of fiscal value per year.

Real-World Numbers in Montevideo (2026)

  • $150,000 apartment (Pocitos): ~$350–$600/year total
  • $250,000 apartment (Punta Carretas): ~$550–$950/year total
  • $500,000 house (Carrasco): ~$900–$1,800/year total

In coastal beach towns like Punta del Este or La Paloma, rates and fiscal values vary by department (Maldonado and Rocha, respectively) but remain similarly low.

No Capital Gains Tax on Real Estate

Uruguay does not impose a capital gains tax on real estate sales for individuals. When you sell, the profit is yours — no percentage owed to the government on appreciation. This is a meaningful structural advantage over Colombia (15% ganancia ocasional) and Panama (10% capital gains), and a major reason investors treat Uruguay as a long-hold market.

Closing Costs at Purchase

One-time taxes at closing are straightforward:

  • Impuesto a las Transmisiones Patrimoniales (ITP): 2% of purchase price, split 1% buyer / 1% seller
  • Notary fees: ~3% of purchase price (regulated; paid by buyer)
  • Registration fees: ~1% of purchase price

All-in, buyers should budget roughly 5–6% of purchase price in closing costs — slightly higher than Colombia or Panama but still well below most European markets.

The 11-Year Tax Holiday for New Residents

Under Law 20.075, foreigners who establish Uruguayan tax residency can elect to pay zero Uruguayan income tax on foreign-source income for 11 years. This does not reduce the Contribución Inmobiliaria (which is a property tax, not income tax), but it eliminates Uruguayan tax on rental income from properties held outside the country, dividends, and other overseas earnings during the holiday period. If you are buying in Uruguay and planning to relocate, the residency path is worth understanding before you arrive.

Bottom Line

Uruguay's property tax burden for foreigners is genuinely low: annual holding costs under 0.60% of fiscal value, no capital gains tax on exit, and a straightforward legal framework that treats foreign buyers identically to citizens. Closing costs run 5–6% — budget for them carefully. And if residency is on the table, the 11-year income tax holiday adds a layer of tax efficiency that few countries match.
