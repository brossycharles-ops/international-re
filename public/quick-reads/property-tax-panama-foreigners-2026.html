Property Tax in Panama for Foreigners 2026

By Charles Brossy May 13, 2026 3 min read

Panama treats foreign property owners exactly like citizens when it comes to annual tax — no surcharge, no nationality-based restrictions. Primary residences valued under $120,000 pay zero property tax, and the top rate for investment properties caps out at just 0.8% per year. Here is the full picture for 2026.

The Rate Table

Panama's property tax is levied on cadastral value — the government's assessed value, which typically runs 10–25% below market price in urban areas. The standard rates for non-primary-residence properties are:

  • Up to $30,000: 0% (exempt)
  • $30,001 – $250,000: 0.6% per year
  • Above $250,000: 0.8% per year

Register the property as your primary residence (patrimonio familiar) and the exemption threshold rises to $120,000, with a reduced rate applying above that level. On a $200,000 primary-residence property, the annual tax bill is typically under $500.

What Changed on January 1, 2026

For years, buyers of new construction in Panama benefited from a 5- to 20-year property tax holiday on improvements, a powerful incentive that drove condo pre-sales. Law 468 of April 2025 eliminated this exemption for new residential properties sold after December 31, 2025. If you close on a new-build in 2026 or later, annual tax applies from day one. Properties sold before that date retain their existing exemption period.

This change has no retroactive effect, but it does shift the math for investors buying off-plan — factor it into your yield projections from the start.

Transfer Tax and Closing Costs

Annual property tax is only part of the picture. When you buy in Panama, expect a 2% transfer tax on the higher of purchase price or cadastral value, plus legal and notary fees totalling roughly 1–2%. All-in, closing costs run about 3–5% of the purchase price — competitive by Latin American standards.

Bottom Line

Panama remains one of the lowest property-tax jurisdictions in Latin America. The loss of new-construction tax holidays in 2026 is a real change, but ongoing annual rates are still a fraction of what US or European investors are used to. Combined with dollarization, no capital gains tax on primary residences, and straightforward foreign ownership rules, the fiscal case for Panama property is intact.
