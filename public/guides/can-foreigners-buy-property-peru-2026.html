Can Foreigners Buy Property in Peru? Yes: 2026 Rules and Costs

By Charles Brossy October 20, 2026 8 min read

Yes, foreigners can buy property in Peru, in their own name and on the same legal terms as Peruvians. Article 71 of the 1993 Constitution puts foreign individuals and companies “in the same condition as Peruvians” regarding property. The one real restriction is geographic: within 50 kilometers of Peru’s borders, foreigners cannot acquire land unless a supreme decree declares a public necessity. Lima, Arequipa and Cusco all lie well outside that zone. You buy through a notarized public deed, register it with SUNARP, and pay a 3% transfer tax (alcabala) on the price above S/ 55,000 in 2026.

What the Constitution Says, and the Border-Zone Exception

Article 71 has two parts. The first gives foreigners, “whether natural or legal persons”, the same property position as Peruvians, with one condition: they can never invoke an exception or diplomatic protection. In practice, a property dispute is settled in Peruvian courts under Peruvian law; you cannot ask your own government to intervene on your behalf.

The second part is the restriction. Within 50 km of the borders, foreigners may not acquire or possess, “by any title”, mines, lands, forests, waters, fuels or energy sources, directly or indirectly, individually or through a company, on pain of losing the right to the state. The only exception is a case of public necessity expressly declared by a supreme decree approved by the Council of Ministers. That covers holding the land through a Peruvian company too, so a corporate structure is not a workaround. If a property you are considering is anywhere near the Ecuador, Colombia, Brazil, Bolivia or Chile borders (Tumbes, parts of Piura, Tacna and the Amazon regions), have a lawyer confirm the distance before you sign anything.

How the Purchase Works, Step by Step

  1. Check the title before you commit. Ask SUNARP, the national public registry, for the property’s certificado literal (a copy of the registry entry, or partida) and the Certificado Registral Inmobiliario (CRI). The CRI describes the property, shows the registered ownership over the past 10 years and the liens, encumbrances and annotations in force over the past 30 years, and lists titles still pending registration, such as blocks, mortgages and transfers. It can be requested online through SUNARP’s Servicio de Publicidad Registral en Línea and arrives by email.
  2. Sign the contract (minuta). Peru’s notary law, Legislative Decree 1049, says the body of a public deed contains the parties’ declaration of will “contained in a minuta authorized by a lawyer”, which the notary inserts word for word. So your lawyer, ideally one who represents only you, drafts or reviews the minuta and signs it.
  3. Sign the public deed (escritura pública) before a notary. Buyer and seller sign before a Peruvian notary, who issues the parte notarial, the official copy used for registration. If you cannot be in Peru, ask your lawyer about granting a power of attorney; how it must be executed and legalized abroad is a point to confirm with the notary in advance.
  4. Pay the alcabala to the municipality (SAT in the province of Lima) by the last business day of the month after the transfer. See the cost table below.
  5. Register the deed in SUNARP’s Registro de Predios. The request is filed through the Sistema de Intermediación Digital (SID-SUNARP), normally by the notary. A registrar reviews the title and either registers the sale, issues an observation if something is missing, or issues a fee assessment if registration fees are unpaid. Your purchase appears in the property’s partida only once that is done, so do not treat a signed deed as the finish line.

Closing Costs and Annual Taxes in 2026

Peru’s tax unit, the UIT, is S/ 5,500 for 2026 (Supreme Decree 301-2025-EF). Several property taxes are set in UIT, so the sol figures below change each year.

Worked example: on a S/ 750,000 resale apartment, the exempt slice is S/ 55,000, so alcabala is 3% of S/ 695,000 = S/ 20,850, about 2.8% of the price. Our Peru alcabala explainer walks through another example and the due date.

Annual property tax (impuesto predial). It is charged by the district municipality on the assessed value of all your properties in that district, not on the price you paid, using a cumulative progressive scale:

On an assessed value of S/ 600,000, that works out to S/ 165 + S/ 1,485 + S/ 2,700 = S/ 4,350 a year. Municipalities may set a minimum of 0.6% of the UIT (S/ 33 in 2026). You can pay in one installment by the last business day of February or in four quarterly installments (February, May, August, November). As the buyer, you become the taxpayer from 1 January of the year after your purchase. The law also requires a sworn declaration (declaración jurada) to be filed with the municipality by the last business day of the month after any transfer; ask your notary or lawyer to handle it. Municipalities also charge arbitrios, fees for local public services provided to each property, which are set by each district’s ordinance, so ask the seller for recent receipts to see the actual amount.

Tax ID and Taxes When You Sell

Ask your notary which identity document they need from you (a foreign buyer typically signs with a passport or, if resident, a carné de extranjería), and ask a Peruvian accountant early whether you need a taxpayer number (RUC). That matters most if you plan to rent the property out and will owe Peruvian tax on the rent. When a non-resident individual later sells, SUNAT says a buyer domiciled in Peru must withhold the seller’s income tax at 5% and pay it through Virtual Form 617. Legislative Decree 1258 cut that rate from 30% to 5% from 1 January 2017. How the taxable amount is calculated and what you can deduct are questions for a Peruvian tax adviser, along with how any Peruvian tax is credited in your home country.

Does Owning Property Give You Residency?

No. Peru has no residency category for buying a home. The two categories buyers ask about work differently:

  • Investor (inversionista): Migraciones requires proof of an investment of at least S/ 500,000, made by international transfer in a single disbursement through a bank supervised by Peru’s banking regulator, into a new or existing company registered with SUNARP. The company must show it is operating (tax return, municipal operating license). The initial stay is 365 days, renewable. A home bought in your own name does not qualify.
  • Rentista: for people with a lifetime pension or permanent income. Applicants with foreign-source income must show permanent net income of at least US$1,000 a month.

Check the current requirements and fees on Migraciones’ pages on gob.pe before applying, since the regulation lets Migraciones set some amounts by administrative act.

Risks to Weigh

Safety. The US State Department rates Peru Level 2: Exercise Increased Caution, citing crime, civil unrest, kidnapping and terrorism; the advisory was reissued on 4 September 2026 without a change in level. It lists “Do Not Travel” areas, including the Colombia–Peru border area in the Loreto region the VRAEM (the valleys of the Apurímac, Ene and Mantaro rivers), and the cities of Abancay, Ayacucho, Huancavelica and Huancayo. Read the full advisory before scouting outside the main cities.

Title fraud and land trafficking. Peru has a long-running problem with tráfico de terrenos, organized groups that seize land and sell it. In one 2018 case, police and prosecutors broke up a gang in Lurín, south of Lima, that had taken state land and sold it to unsuspecting buyers. The defenses are the ones above: buy only property registered in SUNARP in the seller’s name, pull a fresh CRI, use your own lawyer, and make sure the registration is completed. After you buy, SUNARP offers two free tools: Alerta Registral, which emails or texts you when a title is filed against your partida, and a voluntary inmovilización (temporary block) of the partida for up to 10 years, useful for owners who live abroad. Use only the official site, alertaregistral.sunarp.gob.pe.

Currency. Peru’s currency is the sol (PEN), but Lima apartment prices are commonly discussed in US dollars: Peru’s central bank tracks asking prices collected from the Urbania portal in current dollars as well as in constant soles. The difference shows: in the fourth quarter of 2025 the central bank’s 12-district index of asking prices rose 5.8% on the quarter in current dollars but only 1.3% in constant soles. Taxes such as alcabala and predial are computed in soles. Keep the exchange rate you used, and plan your transfer of funds (see our guide on how to transfer money abroad to buy property).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a US citizen buy property in Peru?

Yes. Article 71 of Peru's 1993 Constitution puts foreigners, individuals or companies, in the same position as Peruvians with respect to property. The one exception is land within 50 kilometers of Peru's borders, where foreigners cannot acquire property unless a supreme decree approved by the Council of Ministers declares a public necessity. Lima and the other main cities are not affected.

Does buying property in Peru give you residency?

No. Owning a home is not itself a route to residency. The investor residence category requires an investment of at least S/ 500,000 in a Peruvian company registered with SUNARP, not a home purchase, and the rentista category requires a lifetime pension or, for foreign-source income, at least US$1,000 a month in permanent net income.

How much is the property transfer tax in Peru in 2026?

The alcabala is 3% of the price, paid only by the buyer, with the first 10 UIT exempt. With the 2026 UIT at S/ 5,500, the exempt slice is S/ 55,000, so on a S/ 750,000 apartment the tax is S/ 20,850. It is due by the last business day of the month after the transfer. A builder's first sale of a new unit pays alcabala only on the land value.
