Can Foreigners Buy Property in Chile? A Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy June 2, 2026 8 min read

The short answer: yes — and with fewer structural hurdles than most of Latin America. Chile imposes no restricted coastal zones like Mexico, no mandatory bank trusts, and no caps on foreign ownership of urban or rural land tied to your nationality. A foreigner can hold full, direct title to an apartment in Santiago, a house in Vitacura, or a vineyard estate in the Maipo Valley in exactly the same way a Chilean citizen would. The one administrative step that trips most buyers up — the RUT — is not a legal barrier at all. It takes a few days and costs nothing.

The Legal Framework: Equal Rights for Foreign Buyers

Chile's civil code and its bilateral investment treaties establish that foreign nationals enjoy the same property rights as Chilean citizens. There is no structural discrimination based on passport. Unlike Mexico's Restricted Zone rules — which require a bank fideicomiso for coastal purchases — Chilean law places no nationality-based limits on where or what you can buy. The restrictions that do apply in Chile are asset-based (indigenous lands, national parks) or geography-based for specific nationalities, not blanket prohibitions on foreigners.

The one geographic carve-out to know about is DIFROL — the Dirección de Fronteras y Límites. Nationals of bordering countries (Argentina, Bolivia, and Peru) may need prior authorization from DIFROL to purchase property in designated strategic border zones. This is a security provision, not a restriction on foreigners generally, and it applies only in specific areas within 10–50 km of Chile's land borders. For most buyers — and for any property in Santiago, Valparaíso, or the coastal resort towns — DIFROL is irrelevant.

Step 1 — Get a RUT (Chilean Tax ID)

Before any deed can be registered in your name, you need a Chilean RUT (Rol Único Tributario), the tax identification number issued by the Servicio de Impuestos Internos (SII). The good news: you do not need a visa or Chilean residency to obtain one. Even a tourist can apply — the SII accepts online applications with a valid passport and a Chilean address (your attorney's office qualifies). Processing takes two to five business days and is free. Your attorney typically handles this alongside due diligence, so it adds no meaningful delay to your timeline.

The Buying Process, Step by Step

Due Diligence

Engage a Chilean property attorney to pull the property's certificado de dominio vigente from the Conservador de Bienes Raíces — Chile's public property registry. This document shows the chain of title, any liens, mortgages, or encumbrances, and confirms the seller actually owns the property. Your attorney will also verify that annual property taxes (contribuciones) are current, since outstanding tax debt can travel with the property. Budget $800–$2,000 USD for a competent bilingual attorney in Santiago or Valparaíso.

Promise Agreement and Deposit

Parties typically sign a promesa de compraventa — a binding purchase agreement — locking in price, terms, and a closing date. The buyer pays a deposit at signing, usually 10% of the purchase price. The promesa is legally enforceable; walking away without cause forfeits the deposit.

The Public Deed and Registration

The final transfer is executed as an escritura pública before a Chilean notary. Both buyer and seller (or their attorneys-in-fact with a notarized power of attorney) must appear. The notary calculates fees and certifies the deed. Legal ownership transfers only upon inscription at the Conservador de Bienes Raíces — registration typically takes 5–15 business days. Until the inscription is confirmed and a new certificado de dominio vigente shows your name, the transaction is not legally complete.

Closing Costs: What to Budget

Chile has no traditional property transfer tax on residential purchases, which keeps buyer-side closing costs lean by regional standards. For a cash purchase, plan for total buyer costs of 2–3% of the purchase price. With a mortgage, costs rise to 3–4.5% due to stamp tax on the loan documents and appraisal fees. The main line items:

  • Notary fees: Approximately 0.5–1% of the property value, split between buyer and seller.
  • Registry inscription fee (Conservador de Bienes Raíces): Roughly 0.5–1% of the deed value, paid by the buyer.
  • Attorney fees: $800–$2,000 USD depending on transaction complexity.
  • Stamp tax (Impuesto de Timbres y Estampillas): Applies only to mortgage loans — 0.8% of the loan amount. Cash purchases are exempt.
  • Capital gains (seller's liability): 10% on the profit above the acquisition cost, with a lifetime exemption of 8,000 UF (~USD 280,000) available to individual sellers. This is the seller's tax — not yours as a buyer — but it shapes seller pricing and negotiation.

Annual Property Tax: Modest and Indexed

Chile's annual property tax (contribuciones de bienes raíces) is levied by the SII on the fiscal assessed value (avalúo fiscal), which typically runs at 50–70% of market value. Statutory rates are 1.2% on fiscal values up to approximately CLP 56.8 million, and 1.4% on amounts above that threshold. In practice, the effective tax on a mid-range Santiago apartment is modest: a property with a market value of USD 200,000 might carry a fiscal value of USD 110,000 and a yearly tax bill of around USD 1,320–1,540, paid in four quarterly instalments. Foreigners pay the same rates as Chilean nationals — no surcharges apply.

Restrictions to Know About

The assets you cannot buy as a foreigner (or as anyone) in Chile are specific and narrow:

  • Tierras indígenas: Land protected under Chile's Indigenous Law is inalienable and cannot be transferred to non-indigenous buyers. Any contract in violation is null by operation of law.
  • National parks and protected reserves: Private ownership within CONAF-managed protected areas is prohibited.
  • Border zone properties (DIFROL): Nationals of Argentina, Bolivia, and Peru need DIFROL authorization for purchases in specific frontier areas. Other nationalities are unaffected.

For urban apartments, coastal condos, and agricultural or vineyard estates outside protected areas — the assets most foreign buyers are targeting — none of these restrictions apply. Does Buying Property Lead to Residency?

Not automatically. Unlike Colombia or Panama, Chile does not offer a direct real estate investor visa tied to a minimum purchase price. Chile's investor temporary residency category requires a minimum investment of USD 500,000 directed toward an economic activity that produces goods or services — a commercial development, a tourism project, or a business venture — plus an official sponsorship letter from InvestChile, the government's investment promotion agency. Buying a residential apartment for personal use or rental income does not qualify on its own.

That said, foreigners who live in Chile on other visa categories (work visa, pensioner visa, or after an initial tourist stay) can qualify for permanent residency after one year of temporary residency. Property ownership is not the pathway — but Chile's residency tracks are among the most accessible in the region once you're in the country.

The Bottom Line

Chile is one of Latin America's cleanest property markets for foreign buyers: no structural complexity, no mandatory trusts, no coastal zone carve-outs. Get your RUT early, work with a bilingual Chilean attorney, and the legal process is as close to plug-and-play as the region offers. The annual property tax is reasonable, closing costs are lean, and title security through the Conservador de Bienes Raíces is well-established.
