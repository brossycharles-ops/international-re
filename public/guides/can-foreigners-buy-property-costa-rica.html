Can Foreigners Buy Property in Costa Rica? A Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy April 14, 2026 · Updated October 19, 2026 10 min read

The short answer: yes. Article 19 of the Costa Rican Constitution gives foreigners the same individual and social rights as Costa Ricans, subject to the exceptions set by the Constitution and the law. In practice that means you can buy titled property in your own name with no local partner and no residency requirement. The big exception is the beach: land within 200 meters of the high-tide line is mostly state-owned and can only be held by concession, which foreigners generally cannot get. Every figure below was checked against an official or established source on October 19, 2026; the sources are listed at the end.

The Legal Foundation: Equal Rights for Foreign Buyers

Article 19 of the Constitution puts foreigners on the same footing as citizens, with only the exceptions the Constitution and the law set. The US State Department's 2025 Investment Climate Statement puts it plainly: foreigners are subject to the same land acquisition laws as Costa Ricans, apart from concessions in the Maritime Zone.

You can buy in your personal name, in the name of a Costa Rican company (more on that below), or jointly with a spouse or partner. Title is recorded in the Registro Nacional (National Registry), where recording of title and mortgages is mandatory. The registered owner, mortgages, liens and other annotations can be checked through the Registry, and a notary can pull a certified copy online.

Understanding Property Types: The Critical Distinction

Not all Costa Rican property is created equal. Before diving into the buying process, you need to understand the difference between titled land and coastal land in the Maritime Zone, because the legal rules are very different.

1. Titled Property (Propiedad con Título)

This is standard freehold real estate, similar to fee-simple ownership in the US or Canada. The property has its own registered title and survey plan (plano catastrado) in the National Registry. Foreigners can buy titled property on the same terms as citizens. This is the safest category and what most buyers should target.

2. Maritime Zone Property (Zona Marítimo Terrestre)

This is where things get complicated. Costa Rica's Maritime Zone Law (Law No. 6043 of 1977) covers a strip 200 meters wide along both coasts, measured from the ordinary high-tide line. It is divided into two sections:

  • Public Zone (first 50 meters): Public land. No one, citizen or foreigner, can own it or build on it, and it guarantees public beach access.
  • Restricted Zone (the next 150 meters): Also owned by the state and cannot be bought by anyone. The municipality can grant a concession, which is a lease for a set period and use: Article 48 sets a term of no less than five and no more than 20 years, and Article 50 allows successive extensions with municipal approval and approval of the ICT or the land institute.

The practical implication: that beachfront lot with 30 meters to the waves is almost certainly in the Maritime Zone, apart from a few properties duly registered before the law was passed. Article 47 of Law 6043 bars concessions for foreigners who have not lived in Costa Rica for at least five years, for companies domiciled abroad or set up in Costa Rica by foreigners, and for entities more than 50% foreign-owned. Concession-holding companies also cannot transfer shares to foreigners, and transfers that break this rule are void. Treat any "nominee company" workaround as a serious legal risk and get independent advice. Ask explicitly whether any property you're considering falls within 200 meters of the high-tide line.

3. Concession Land

Some coastal developments sit on Maritime Zone concessions rather than titled land. A concession is a lease from the state, not ownership, and under Article 45 transferring one needs the approval of the municipality and of the Costa Rican Tourism Institute (ICT) or the land institute, as applicable. Confirm the concession's validity, remaining term, permitted use and transferability with your own attorney before making any offer.

The Buying Process, Step by Step

Step 1: Hire Your Own Attorney-Notary

In Costa Rica the transfer deed (escritura) is executed before a notario público, who is always a licensed attorney. The notary prepares the deed, collects the taxes and fees, and records the deed at the Registro Nacional. The State Department notes that the Registry has no legal authority to question notarial acts beyond procedural formalities, which is why you need an attorney who works for you.

Do not use the seller's attorney. Hire your own, independently of the seller and the listing agent, and ask for a written quote for fees and expenses before you start.

Step 2: Conduct Due Diligence

Your attorney should run a title study at the National Registry. This verifies:

  • The seller is the true registered owner
  • Any liens, mortgages, annotations or encumbrances on the title
  • Property taxes are current (the seller normally provides a municipal tax clearance certificate)
  • The registered survey plan (plano catastrado) matches the physical property boundaries
  • No legal proceedings or lawsuits affect the title

Beyond the registry search, conduct on-the-ground diligence: verify water availability (a construction permit needs a water availability letter from the local ASADA or from AyA, the national water utility), zoning (uso de suelo), legal access, and whether any construction on the property was properly permitted. The State Department also warns that long-time occupants can sometimes claim possession, so inspect the land in person.

Step 3: Sign a Purchase Agreement and Use Escrow

Once due diligence is satisfactory, buyer and seller sign an opción de compra (purchase option) or purchase agreement setting the price, deposit, conditions, closing date and penalties for breach. The deposit amount is negotiable, so settle it in writing.

Deposits and purchase funds should go through an escrow agent registered with SUGEF, Costa Rica's financial regulator; the Tico Times puts the cost at about 0.25% or a flat fee of around $1,000. Sending funds directly to a seller without escrow protection is a significant risk.

Step 4: Execute the Transfer Deed

The final closing occurs before the notary, who prepares and authenticates the public deed. Both buyer and seller (or their attorneys-in-fact with power of attorney) sign, and the notary then presents the deed to the National Registry. In the World Bank's last benchmark (Doing Business 2020, May 2019 data), registration took 7 days and the five official transfer steps took 11 days for a clean title in San José; a deed with defects is returned to the notary for correction and refiling. Your ownership is secure against third parties once the deed is recorded in your name. For a step-by-step look at how long each stage takes, see how long it takes to buy property in Costa Rica.

Closing Costs: What to Budget

For a straightforward residential purchase, the Tico Times puts total closing costs at roughly 3.5–5% of the purchase price, more with bank financing or a corporate structure. The main components:

  • Transfer tax: 1.5% (Law 6999, Article 8), on the sale price or the property's registered fiscal value, whichever is greater
  • Stamps and registry fees: the World Bank's 2019 benchmark itemises a 0.5% Registry stamp, a 0.2% municipal stamp and a 0.15% agrarian stamp plus small fixed fees; the Tico Times puts stamps and recording together at roughly 0.5–0.8%
  • Notary fees: set by the official fee schedule (arancel) as a sliding minimum; the World Bank's 2019 benchmark, based on the 2015 schedule, gave 2% on the first ₡11 million of value down to 1% above ₡33 million, plus 13% VAT on the fee (confirm the current scale with your notary); the Tico Times reports 1–2% of the price in practice
  • Escrow: about 0.25% or a flat fee near $1,000

By law (Law 6999, Article 6) the 1.5% transfer tax is owed half by the seller and half by the buyer, and each is jointly liable for all of it. How the other costs are split is a matter of custom and negotiation, so settle it in the purchase agreement. Because transfer tax is charged on the higher of the price and the registered value, under-declaring the price in the deed does not reliably reduce it and creates problems for you at resale.

Annual Property Tax: Low by Design

Costa Rica's annual property tax (Law 7509) is 0.25% of the property's appraised value as registered with the municipality. A property registered at $400,000 would pay $1,000 a year. The tax is paid to the municipality where the property sits.

Higher-end homes also pay the Luxury Home Tax (Impuesto Solidario, Law 8683). For 2026, Decree 45358-H set the exempt amount at ₡143 million, measured on the fiscal value of the construction and fixed installations. If a home is over that amount, the land is added and the tax is charged on the whole fiscal value of the home plus land, not just the part above ₡143 million. The scale is progressive: 0.25% on value up to ₡359 million, then 0.30%, 0.35%, 0.40%, 0.45% and 0.50% on successive bands, and 0.55% on value above ₡2,162 million. The amounts are updated every December in line with inflation; the tax is due by January 15 each year. Our Costa Rica property tax quick-read has a worked example and the filing rules.

Corporate Ownership: SRL vs. Sociedad Anónima

Some buyers purchase through a Costa Rican company rather than in their personal name. The two standard structures are the Sociedad Anónima (SA) and the Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada (SRL/Ltda). A company can make sense if you hold several properties, have partners or run a rental business, but it carries ongoing costs:

  • Formation: the minimum notarial fee for setting up an ordinary company under the arancel is ₡181,500 plus VAT, before registry fees and stamps
  • Annual tax on legal entities (Law 9428): for 2026 it is ₡69,330 for a company not registered for income tax and ₡115,550 for one with no activity or low income, rising to ₡231,100 for companies with higher income. It is owed whether or not the company is active
  • Beneficial ownership reporting: companies must file an annual declaration of their owners with the Central Bank (Law 9416)

Selling the company's shares instead of the property does not avoid the 1.5% transfer tax. Since Law 9069 (2012), Law 6999 treats any transfer of control over a company that owns real estate as an indirect transfer that triggers the tax. Buying an existing company also means you inherit its liabilities, so have your attorney audit the company alongside the property title.

Financing Options for Foreign Buyers

Local bank financing is hard to get for non-residents. According to the Tico Times, Costa Rican banks generally lend only to legal residents, at loan-to-value ratios well below North American norms and on long approval timelines. Most foreign buyers pay cash or arrange financing in their home country (home equity loans, portfolio loans against existing assets) and wire funds to escrow. Some developers offer stage payments during construction on new-build projects; read those contracts with your attorney, since your money is at risk until the unit is delivered and titled.

Can Buying Property Lead to Residency?

Not automatically. Owning property does not give you residency; you need a permit from the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería (DGME). The Investor (inversionista) category traditionally required $200,000. Law 9996 of 2021 lowered the minimum to $150,000, including real estate, "for the period the law sets", and its incentives were available to applicants in the law's first five years, a window that ended in July 2026. Reports in September 2026 said DGME's own page still listed $150,000, but whether the lower figure continues has not been officially clarified, so confirm the threshold with DGME or an immigration attorney before you size a purchase around residency. Temporary residence is renewed every two years, and temporary residents can apply for permanent residence after three years.

Separately, the Rentista category requires at least $2,500 a month of stable income guaranteed for at least two years, certified by a bank (a deposit returned in monthly instalments no longer counts), and the Pensionado category requires a lifetime pension of at least $1,000 a month. Neither needs a property investment, and many retirees combine a purchase with a pensionado application. Rentistas and pensionados cannot work as employees. Maritime Zone concessions remain closed to foreigners until they have lived in the country for five years.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the title search: Title fraud happens. The State Department cites the 2024 "Madre Patria" case, in which a criminal network allegedly used notaries to change deeds and sell absent owners' properties. Verify ownership through the National Registry independently of what the seller shows you, and after closing subscribe to the Registry's e-mail alert service (about $15 a year).
  • Ignoring Maritime Zone status: Beachfront doesn't mean titled. Confirm on the registered survey whether any portion of the lot falls within 200 meters of high tide.
  • Sending money without escrow: Wire funds only to a SUGEF-registered escrow agent, never directly to a seller or broker.
  • Accepting unpermitted construction or no water: Verify that structures have municipal permits, and get the water availability letter before money moves.
  • Not checking tax arrears: Get the municipal tax clearance certificate and confirm a zero balance before closing.
  • Assuming a share deal saves tax: Buying the seller's company still triggers the 1.5% transfer tax and leaves you with the company's history.

The Bottom Line

Foreigners can buy titled property in Costa Rica in their own name on the same terms as citizens, with a public registry, a 0.25% annual property tax and closing costs of roughly 3.5–5%. The Maritime Zone is the real limit: the first 200 meters from the high-tide line is state land, and concessions there are closed to foreigners with under five years of residence. Budget for the luxury home tax if the house is expensive, since it applies to the full value once you are over the threshold, and don't count on a company structure to avoid transfer tax.

The single most important step: hire an independent attorney-notary before you sign anything or wire any funds, use SUGEF-registered escrow, and register for the Registry's title alerts once you close. If residency is part of the plan, confirm the current investor threshold with DGME before you buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners own property in Costa Rica in their own name?

Yes. Article 19 of Costa Rica's Constitution gives foreigners the same rights as citizens, with exceptions set by law. Titled property can be bought in your personal name, through a Costa Rican company or jointly, with no local partner or residency required.

Can a foreigner buy beachfront land in Costa Rica?

Generally not within 200 meters of the high-tide line. Under the Maritime Zone Law (Law 6043), the first 50 meters is public land, and the next 150 meters belongs to the state and can only be held by municipal concession of up to 20 years. Article 47 bars concessions for foreigners with under five years of residence and for companies more than 50% foreign-owned.

How do I check that a Costa Rican seller really owns the property?

Have your own independent attorney-notary run a title study at the Registro Nacional, where title recording is mandatory. It should confirm the registered owner, mortgages and liens, that municipal taxes are paid and that the plano catastrado matches the land. After closing, subscribe to the Registry's title alert service.
