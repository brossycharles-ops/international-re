Buenos Aires Rental Yield Analysis 2026: What Investors Are Actually Earning

By Charles Brossy June 5, 2026 9 min read

Buenos Aires is pricing in USD but paying rent in a recovering market. Two years after President Milei repealed Argentina's suffocating rent control law, rental supply has more than tripled, real rents have fallen sharply, and the city has quietly become one of the more interesting yield plays in Latin America — if you know which neighborhoods to target and what costs to model.

The Deregulation Effect: Why This Market Is Different Now

To understand today's yields, you need to understand what happened in December 2023. Milei's government repealed Argentina's 2020 Rental Law, which had mandated three-year fixed-term leases with government-set inflation adjustment formulas. Landlords had responded by pulling units from the long-term market entirely — supply on Zonaprop, Argentina's largest real estate portal, had collapsed to around 5,500 listings in Buenos Aires at the time of repeal.

The market's response to deregulation was immediate. Within six months, listed rental units on Zonaprop surged to over 15,300 — a 180% increase. Across the wider metropolitan area, supply was up more than 200%. With this flood of inventory, real (inflation-adjusted) rents fell approximately 27% in the first seven months after the law's repeal, even as nominal prices climbed with inflation. For tenants, this was a windfall. For landlords collecting in USD — which most foreign investors do — it's a market that has normalized after years of dysfunction.

The result heading into mid-2026: a liquid rental market, transparent pricing, and a clear spread between what you pay per square meter to buy and what you can earn per month to rent. Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Yield Breakdown

Buenos Aires is a city of extreme variation. Puerto Madero and Palermo command luxury premiums; outer barrios like Villa Urquiza and Caballito offer working-class demand with lower entry prices. Here's how the numbers break down as of June 2026.

Puerto Madero — ~$6,100/sqm | Gross Yield: ~3.0–3.5%

The waterfront towers of Puerto Madero are the city's prestige play and its worst-yielding neighborhood. At roughly $6,100 per square meter, these are among the most expensive apartments in South America. Rents are high in absolute terms — a two-bedroom with river views can fetch $2,000–$2,500/month — but the math rarely pencils above 3.5% gross. Factor in high expensas (monthly building fees, covered below) and you're looking at net yields in the 2–2.5% range. Puerto Madero is a wealth-preservation and lifestyle purchase, not an income play.

Palermo — ~$3,400/sqm | Gross Yield: ~4.0–5.0%

Palermo remains the city's most liquid neighborhood for foreign investors — deep demand from both long-term tenants and short-term platforms, good resale liquidity, and a brand-name barrio that is easy to explain to buyers in Miami or Madrid. Entry pricing of around $3,400/sqm puts a renovated one-bedroom in the $120,000–$180,000 range. Gross yields run 4.0–5.0%, with the higher end achievable in older buildings where purchase prices are softer. Palermo Soho and Palermo Hollywood command the tightest cap rates; Palermo Viejo edges slightly higher.

Recoleta — ~$3,200/sqm | Gross Yield: ~4.5–5.5%

Recoleta sits slightly below Palermo on price but punches above it on rental demand from long-term corporate and professional tenants. Classic pre-war apartment buildings in good condition can yield 4.5–5.5% gross, particularly in the blocks near Avenida Santa Fe and Callao. The neighborhood's central location, parks, and established expat community sustain occupancy rates. A key consideration: older buildings here carry higher maintenance costs and expensas, which can erode the yield advantage versus newer Palermo product.

Belgrano — ~$3,000–$3,500/sqm | Gross Yield: ~5.0–6.0%

Belgrano is where the value proposition sharpens. Prices of $3,000–$3,500/sqm reflect a neighborhood with excellent infrastructure — multiple subway lines, a large commercial corridor, good schools — but without Palermo's tourist premium. Rental demand is driven by local professionals and families, making occupancy stable. Well-positioned two-bedrooms can achieve gross yields of 5.5–6.0%, and the neighborhood's relative undervaluation versus Palermo makes it the most frequently cited "yield over prestige" pick among local brokers.

Núñez / Villa Urquiza — ~$2,200–$2,800/sqm | Gross Yield: ~6.0–7.0%

The northern residential barrios — Núñez, Villa Urquiza, Colegiales — represent the highest-yielding accessible tier for foreign investors. Entry prices range from roughly $2,200–$2,800/sqm, and gross yields of 6.0–7.0% are achievable on well-selected units near transit. These neighborhoods lack the international name recognition of Palermo, but they are middle-class strongholds with genuine organic demand and lower expensas from smaller, older buildings. The trade-off is liquidity: exit times can run longer, and you will need local management.

The Expensas Trap: Why Gross Yield Is a Starting Point, Not a Conclusion

Buenos Aires has a property ownership cost that surprises nearly every foreign buyer: expensas. These are mandatory monthly building maintenance fees — covering doormen, elevator contracts, cleaning staff, building insurance, and reserves — and they are non-negotiable obligations of ownership, whether or not your unit is occupied.

In premium buildings (Puerto Madero, modern Palermo towers), expensas can run $400–$800/month per unit, representing 15–30% of gross rental income. In older mid-range buildings, expect $150–$350/month. Even in lower-cost neighborhoods, expensas of $100–$200/month are standard. Before accepting any advertised gross yield, subtract expensas — in premium buildings, this adjustment alone can take a 4.5% gross yield down to a 3.2% net.

The standard modelling approach: take gross yield, deduct expensas (as a percentage of gross rent), deduct property tax (low in Argentina — roughly 0.5–1.0% of assessed value annually), and apply a 90–92% occupancy assumption. For Belgrano and Recoleta, this typically delivers net yields of 3.5–4.5%. For the outer barrios, 4.5–5.5% net is achievable.

Short-Term Rentals: The Airbnb Overlay

Buenos Aires is a top-ten Airbnb market in Latin America, and Palermo, San Telmo, and Recoleta generate consistent short-term demand from the city's large inbound tourism and business travel sector. For owners willing to operate or contract a local management company, short-term yields in these neighborhoods can run 8–12% gross — materially above long-term equivalents.

The caveats are real: Buenos Aires requires local registration for short-term rentals, management fees typically run 20–25% of revenue, and the strategy requires furnished, well-maintained units that trade at a $30,000–$50,000 premium per apartment over unfurnished stock. The economics work best for investors who hold a premium Palermo or Recoleta unit in a building where doormen and building management are cooperative — resistance from building administrations is a recurring friction point.

What You're Buying Per Dollar

To put this in concrete terms: a $200,000 budget in Buenos Aires in mid-2026 buys you a one-bedroom apartment in Belgrano or Recoleta of approximately 60–70 square meters, typically in a building from the 1970s–1990s with elevator and doorman. The same budget buys a studio in Palermo's better blocks or, at the outer barrios, a spacious two-bedroom of 80–100 square meters near transit. Foreign buyers purchase in their own name or through a local SRL (LLC equivalent) — there are no restrictions on foreign property ownership in Argentina, and transactions are USD-denominated with dollar-for-dollar title registration.

Closing costs in Buenos Aires run approximately 6–8% of the purchase price, split between buyer and seller by convention, including notary fees, transfer stamp duty, and registration fees. These are standard and well-understood by local brokers.

Risks Worth Modelling

Argentina is Argentina. The macro environment has improved dramatically under the current government — inflation has fallen from triple digits to roughly 30% annually by mid-2026, the official and parallel exchange rates have converged, and the rental market has functionally normalized. But political risk and currency risk remain real. Foreign investors should consider the following:

  • Currency repatriation: Profits can be repatriated in USD, but exchange controls have historically tightened during crises. Ensure you are structuring ownership in a way that preserves dollar access.
  • Counterparty quality: Long-term leases are now freely negotiated, but enforcing eviction in case of non-payment still takes 6–18 months through Argentine courts. A well-drafted lease with a guarantor (garante) or insurance product (seguro de caución) is the standard mitigation.
  • Expensas delinquency: In older buildings, if unit owners fall behind on expensas, the building can deteriorate rapidly. Review the building's expensas history and administration before closing.
  • Political reversal: The deregulation driving today's improved yields is policy-dependent. A future government reversing Milei's rental market reforms is a tail risk worth holding in mind.

The Bottom Line

Buenos Aires is not the highest-yielding market in Latin America — Medellín, Lima, and certain Colombian secondary cities still outperform on raw numbers. But Buenos Aires is the most sophisticated, liquid, and USD-transparent real estate market on the continent, and after two years of post-deregulation normalization, the underlying supply-demand dynamics are the healthiest they've been in a decade.

For investors targeting 4–6% net yield with a decade-long hold, Belgrano and Recoleta represent the strongest risk-adjusted case. For those willing to accept operational complexity in exchange for higher returns, short-term rental overlays in Palermo push the math into double-digit territory. And for pure capital appreciation plays, outer barrios with improving infrastructure remain the contrarian pick.
