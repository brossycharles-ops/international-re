Uruguay vs Chile for Retirees 2026: Costs, Visas and Taxes

By Charles Brossy Updated October 26, 2026 9 min read

Uruguay or Chile for retirement in 2026? Chile is cheaper day to day: Numbeo's October 2026 comparison puts Santiago about 28% below Montevideo including rent. Uruguay is simpler on tax and paperwork: it does not tax a foreign pension and lets you apply for permanent residence directly, while Chile taxes residents on worldwide income after their first three years. Neither country sets a fixed retiree income minimum. Here is what the official rules and current market data say.

Uruguay: Stability, a Pension Tax Exemption and Direct Permanent Residence

Uruguay is small — the 2023 census counted 3,499,451 people — and it had the highest score in Latin America in Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index (73/100, 17th of 182; Chile scored 63, 31st). Healthcare for residents runs largely through mutualistas, private non-profit providers; each sets its own membership price, so get quotes before you budget.

Pension tax. Uruguay's personal income tax (IRPF) applies to Uruguayan-source income plus capital income from foreign entities and assets (dividends, interest and gains on foreign securities). A foreign pension falls in neither group, so a US Social Security or foreign public pension is not taxed in Uruguay. That foreign capital income is generally taxed at 12% for residents — not a 2026 change. What Law 20.446 changed from January 1, 2026 is the tax holiday: new tax residents who were not tax resident in the previous two years can opt to pay the non-resident tax (IRNR) instead of IRPF on that foreign capital income — in practice no Uruguayan tax on it — for the year they become resident plus the next ten. Someone who becomes resident by living in Uruguay more than 183 days a year qualifies without investing; people who become resident through investment use routes such as Uruguayan real estate worth more than UI 12.5 million. Our tax-holiday explainer covers the routes.

Montevideo prices. INGAR's September 2026 index puts Montevideo apartment asking prices at about US$2,841/m² (the median of its neighbourhood medians). The neighbourhoods most foreign buyers look at cost more: about US$3,956/m² in Pocitos, US$4,184/m² in Punta Carretas and US$4,370/m² in Carrasco. Global Property Guide puts Montevideo's average gross rental yield at 5.40% in Q2 2026; the highest barrio yield INGAR reports is about 11.1% gross in Colón, a peripheral, lower-priced area — not where most retirees buy. See our Montevideo rental yield analysis for more.

Punta del Este is Uruguay's luxury beach market and its prices vary enormously by building. Global Property Guide reports the cheapest new apartments from about US$2,500/m² and top towers on the seafront at US$7,000–10,000/m². For context on market size, El Observador reported that Uruguay's total real-estate sales reached at least US$1.17 billion in the first half of 2025 — a national figure, not Punta del Este alone. Note that Uruguay's IRPF taxes most capital income, including gains on selling property, at 12% (art. 37-T7); our Punta del Este guide covers purchase costs and tax.

Chile: Lower Living Costs, a Bigger City, Tax After Year Three

Cost of living. Numbeo's Montevideo–Santiago comparison (updated October 2026) shows living costs including rent about 28.4% lower in Santiago, about 29.8% lower excluding rent, and rents about 22.4% lower. Numbeo is crowd-sourced, so treat these as indicative, not a budget.

Santiago prices. Global Property Guide puts new apartments in Santiago at about US$2,810/m² in Q3 2026, while CNN Chile, using TOCTOC listing data, gives a Gran Santiago average of about 83 UF/m² (≈US$3,520) for new apartments. The eastern districts that foreign buyers favour are more expensive: TOCTOC puts new apartments at about 117 UF/m² in Vitacura (≈US$4,960), 114 UF/m² in Las Condes (≈US$4,830) and 103 UF/m² in Providencia (≈US$4,370). Santiago's average gross yield is 5.01% (GPG, Q3 2026), with one-bedroom units in the historic centre around 5.4%. On the coast, Viña del Mar yields average 3.81%. Our Santiago neighbourhood guide goes district by district.

Residency. Chile's temporary residence for retirees and rentistas (jubilados y rentistas) has no fixed income figure: the migration service (SERMIG) asks for a pension or steady income that covers at least your basic needs, judged against Ministry of Social Development indicators. After at least 24 months of temporary residence you can apply for definitive (permanent) residence — SERMIG says this can be reduced to 12 months, taking income or pensions into account, and long absences extend the wait. Citizenship requires definitive residence and five years of residence.

Taxes: Where the Two Really Differ

For a pure pensioner, Uruguay is the clearer case: a foreign pension is outside IRPF. Chile's Income Tax Law (article 3) taxes a foreigner who settles in Chile only on Chilean-source income for the first three years from arrival, and that period can be extended; after it ends, residents are taxed on worldwide income. Whether your particular pension is then taxable in Chile depends on the tax treaty with your home country, so get advice before year three. Retirees with foreign investment portfolios should also compare Uruguay's 2026 holiday option with Chile's three-year window.

Buying as a Foreigner

Both countries let foreigners buy homes in their own name, with no local partner or residency needed. Two exceptions are worth knowing. In Chile, Decree-Law 1939 bars nationals of neighbouring countries from acquiring property in declared border zones unless specifically authorised. In Uruguay, Law 18.092 limits who can hold rural and farm land to individuals and certain entities, which affects companies buying farmland. Neither affects a city apartment or a beach condo bought in your own name.

The Verdict: Simplicity vs. Budget

Choose Uruguay if you live mainly on a foreign pension and want the simplest tax position, direct permanent residence and the region's best corruption ranking — and can accept higher living costs. FAQ: Uruguay vs Chile for Retirees

How much income do I need to retire in Uruguay or Chile?

Neither country sets a fixed amount. Uruguay asks permanent-residence applicants to show monthly income sufficient to live on, and Chile's SERMIG asks for a pension or steady income that covers at least basic needs. The US$1,500 figure often quoted for Uruguay belongs to a separate Law 16.340 regime, not to ordinary residence.

Is Chile cheaper than Uruguay for retirees?

Yes, on Numbeo's October 2026 comparison: living costs including rent are about 28% lower in Santiago than in Montevideo, and rents about 22% lower. Asking prices per m² in the two capitals are in a similar range (about US$2,800–3,500 for typical apartments), though the sources measure them differently.

Does Uruguay or Chile tax a foreign pension?

Uruguay does not: its IRPF covers Uruguayan-source income and foreign capital income, not foreign pensions. Chile taxes a new resident only on Chilean-source income for the first three years after arrival; after that, residents are taxed on worldwide income, subject to tax treaties.
