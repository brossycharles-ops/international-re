Closing Costs for Foreign Buyers in Latin America 2026: Country Table

By Charles Brossy October 27, 2026 9 min read

Closing costs for foreign buyers in Latin America range from almost nothing in transfer tax for the buyer in Panama, where the 2% transfer tax falls on the seller by law, to 4% in Playa del Carmen, 3% in Peru and 3% in São Paulo, all paid by the buyer. In Uruguay, buyer and seller each pay 2%, and Costa Rica's 1.5% is split in half by law. Notary and registry fees come on top. The table below gives the 2026 figures for 11 countries, each checked against the law, the tax authority or the registry.

How to Read This Table

Rows are ordered roughly from the lowest to the highest statutory transfer tax for the buyer. Three things matter more than the headline rate:

  1. The base. Uruguay charges its 2% on the cadastral valor real, which can differ from the price. Peru, Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, São Paulo and Mexico use the price or an official value, whichever is higher. Ask for the official value early, because it can raise the bill.
  2. Who pays by law, and who pays by custom. Peru puts the alcabala on the buyer "without admitting agreement to the contrary", and Panama's 2026 reform voids any clause that moves its ITBI to the buyer. Costa Rica and Colombia split their taxes in half by law, while Ecuador presumes a split unless the contract says otherwise. Where the law is silent, the purchase contract decides, so negotiate it before you sign.
  3. Fees outside the tax. Notaries in Uruguay, Ecuador and São Paulo charge regulated fees; in Peru, Mexico and much of Central America they quote freely. Ask for a written estimate covering taxes, notary, registry, stamp duties and the lawyer's title review.

Country Notes

Panama. Law 546, published on August 31, 2026, exempts the first B/.120,000 of the transfer-tax base on the first sale of a new home within two years of its occupancy permit, with stepped rates up to B/.200,000. It does not cover resales. See Can Foreigners Buy Property in Panama?

Costa Rica. Because buyer and seller are jointly liable for the full 1.5%, make sure the seller's half is paid at closing. Our Costa Rica transfer tax tip works through a US$300,000 example.

Peru. The 3% alcabala falls on the buyer alone and no contract can change that, but the first 10 UIT are exempt and a developer's first sale is taxed on the land value only. See our Peru alcabala tip.

Ecuador. Our Ecuador alcabala tip explains the presumed split and the resale rebates.

Mexico. Playa del Carmen's revenue law, as reformed in December 2025, sets ISAI at 4%; many older guides still quote 2%. Foreigners buying in the restricted coastal zone also need a bank trust (fideicomiso), which adds set-up and annual fees. See Can Foreigners Buy Property in Mexico?

Dominican Republic. Law 30-26 of June 2026 adds a 10% capital-gains tax on individual sellers and cuts the separate 2% tax on other real-estate operations to 1% in 2027 and abolishes it from 2028, according to PwC's summary; we could not open the official text. Our CONFOTUR tip covers the tourism exemption.

Buenos Aires. The single-home exemption applies to the buyer's share only, and only for a permanent family home that is the buyer's sole property, which rules out most investment purchases.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Latin American country has the lowest closing costs for buyers?

In transfer tax, Panama: its 2% ITBI is paid by the seller by law, and a clause moving it to the buyer is void. Chile charges no transfer tax on a private resale under the SII rules we checked, though the Santiago registry fee (0.2%, capped) and any mortgage stamp tax still apply.

Who pays the transfer tax when buying property in Latin America?

It depends on the country. The buyer pays by law in Peru (3%), São Paulo (3%) and Mexico's municipalities; the seller pays in Panama (2%); buyer and seller split it by law in Costa Rica (1.5%) and Colombia; and in Uruguay each side pays its own 2%.

Do notary fees in Latin America depend on the price?

In some countries. Uruguay's official notarial schedule sets 3% plus VAT, Ecuador's fee rises in bands tied to the basic wage, and São Paulo uses fixed statewide bands. In Peru and much of Central America notaries quote freely, so get a written estimate before signing.
