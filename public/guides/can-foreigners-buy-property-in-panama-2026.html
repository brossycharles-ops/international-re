Can Foreigners Buy Property in Panama? A Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy May 19, 2026 9 min read

The short answer: yes — and with fewer bureaucratic hurdles than almost anywhere else in the region. Panama's constitution grants foreign nationals essentially the same property rights as Panamanian citizens when it comes to titled real estate. There are no mandatory bank trusts (unlike Mexico), no capital-registration requirements (unlike Colombia), and no blanket restrictions on beachfront titled land. A foreigner can hold full, direct fee-simple title to a condominium in Panama City, a house in Coronado, or a titled beach lot in Pedasi in exactly the same way a Panamanian national would. The key distinctions — maritime zone rules, border restrictions, and the difference between titled land and concessions — are what every buyer needs to understand before signing anything.

The Legal Framework

Panama's property laws make no distinction between foreign and domestic buyers for titled property (propiedad titulada). Once a property has a clear finca number registered in the Public Registry (Registro Público de Panamá), it can be freely bought and sold by any natural or legal person, regardless of nationality. Panama uses a dollar-denominated economy with no currency risk, and the judicial system recognizes and enforces foreign ownership without special permits or government approval.

There are two categories of restriction worth knowing:

  • 10-kilometre border zones: Land within 10 km of the borders with Costa Rica and Colombia is subject to additional restrictions on foreign ownership, rooted in national security law. Buying near a designated border zone requires specialist legal guidance.
  • The Maritime Zone: The first 22 metres measured from the mean high-tide line is inalienable public domain — no one can own it. The next 200 metres seaward of that boundary falls within the "maritime zone" and can only be used under a government concesión (concession), not purchased outright. A concession is a leasehold right, not a title — and foreigners cannot hold a concession in their first five years of legal residency. If you are buying a beachfront property, confirm with your attorney whether the land is titled or concession-based. Titled beachfront exists throughout Panama, but due diligence on this point is essential.

The Buying Process, Step by Step

Title Search and Due Diligence

Engage a licensed Panamanian attorney before placing any deposit. Your attorney will search the Public Registry to obtain a certificado de registro confirming the property's finca number, the registered owner, any outstanding mortgages, liens, or encumbrances, and the official cadastral value. They will also verify that property taxes (Impuesto de Inmuebles) are current — unpaid balances follow the property, not the seller. Budget $1,500–$3,000 USD for a competent bilingual property attorney.

Promise Agreement

Once due diligence is satisfactory, buyer and seller sign a promesa de compraventa — a binding purchase agreement that locks in the price, closing date, and conditions. The buyer typically pays a deposit of 10% of the purchase price at this stage. If the seller defaults, the buyer recovers double the deposit; if the buyer defaults, the deposit is forfeited. Both sides should be represented by counsel before signing.

The Public Deed and Transfer Tax

The final transfer deed — the escritura pública — is executed before a Panamanian notary (Notaría Pública). Before the deed is registered, the buyer must pay the 2% transfer tax (ITBI) — calculated on whichever is higher, the purchase price or the official cadastral value. This payment is submitted to the tax authority (DGI) and a receipt is required before the notary will complete registration.

Registration

The notary submits the executed deed to the Public Registry for inscription. Legal ownership transfers only upon registration — until then, the property remains in the seller's name in the government's records. Processing time at the Public Registry is typically 10–20 business days for a standard cash transaction. A clean, unencumbered purchase from agreement to title can close in 30–60 days.

Closing Costs: What to Budget

Panama's closing costs are moderate by regional standards. Total buyer-side transaction costs typically run 4–6% of the purchase price. The main items:

  • Transfer tax (ITBI): 2% of the higher of the purchase price or cadastral value — paid by the buyer.
  • Legal fees: 1–1.5% of the purchase price ($1,500–$3,000 on a $200,000 property).
  • Notary fees: 0.1–0.25% of the transaction value (~$200–$500).
  • Public Registry filing: Nominal fee, typically included within legal fees.
  • Property inspection: $300–$600 for an independent structural survey — strongly recommended.

The seller is responsible for a separate 3% capital gains tax on the sale, withheld at closing. As a buyer, this is not your liability, but it affects how sellers price and negotiate — particularly on properties held for many years.

Annual Property Tax: Low and Tiered

Panama's Impuesto de Inmuebles is based on the official cadastral value and applies equally to foreign and domestic owners. The rates are structured to favour primary residences and modest properties:

  • 0% on the first $120,000 of cadastral value (for primary residences)
  • 0.5% on cadastral value between $120,001 and $700,000
  • 0.7% on cadastral value above $700,000

Tax is payable in three equal instalments: April 30, August 31, and December 31. Because cadastral values frequently run below market value, effective tax rates for most foreign buyers are very low — a $300,000 market-value property with a $180,000 cadastral value would generate an annual bill of roughly $300 USD. New constructions often receive a 20-year exoneración (property tax exemption) on the construction value, which is a meaningful incentive for buyers of new condos.

Can Buying Property Lead to Residency?

Yes — and Panama offers some of the most accessible real-estate-linked residency programmes in Latin America. If you're comparing Panama's thresholds against Colombia's investor visa or Costa Rica's rentista programme, our free market quiz maps these pathways side by side. The two primary routes tied to property investment are:

Friendly Nations Visa

Citizens of 50 "friendly nations" — including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and most of the EU — can qualify for a Visa de Países Amigos with a minimum real estate investment of $200,000 USD in titled property. The visa grants two-year temporary residency, renewable, and is a pathway to permanent residency. Processing typically takes three to six months.

Qualified Investor Visa

Panama's Qualified Investor programme currently requires a minimum real estate investment of $300,000 USD and grants immediate permanent residency — bypassing the temporary stage entirely. Processing takes 30–90 days. Critically, the minimum investment threshold is scheduled to rise to $500,000 USD after October 2026, so buyers targeting this programme should act before that date. Citizenship is available after five years of permanent residency.

Mortgage Financing for Foreigners

Panamanian banks do lend to non-residents, but terms are tighter than for residents. Expect a loan-to-value ratio of 50–70%, meaning a minimum 30–50% down payment. Dollar-denominated mortgage rates in Panama as of mid-2026 range from approximately 6–8.5% — considerably more favourable than the peso-denominated rates found in Colombia or Argentina. The most common banks serving foreign buyers include Banco General, Global Bank, and Banistmo. Most foreign investors complete cash purchases to simplify closing and avoid cross-border lending complexity.

Key Things to Verify Before Signing

  • Titled vs. concession: Any property near the beach must be verified as titled rather than concession-based.
  • Tax exoneration status: Confirm whether a new-build property carries an active exoneración and when it expires.
  • HOA (PH) fees: High-rise condos in Panama City carry monthly fees ranging from $200 to over $1,000; these significantly affect net rental yields.
  • Developer track record: Pre-construction purchases are common in Panama; verify the developer's completion history before paying a deposit.

The Bottom Line

Panama is among the most structurally straightforward property markets in Latin America for foreign buyers. You can own titled property outright, in your own name, from day one — with no currency risk, no capital registration requirements, and a legal system that respects foreign ownership. The rules that matter are practical, not structural: always confirm titled vs. concession status on beachfront land, pay the transfer tax before the deed is submitted, and act before October 2026 if the Qualified Investor visa is on your agenda.
