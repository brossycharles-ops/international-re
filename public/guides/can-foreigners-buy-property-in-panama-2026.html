Can Foreigners Buy Property in Panama? A Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy May 19, 2026 · Updated October 17, 2026 10 min read

The short answer: yes. Foreigners can buy titled property in Panama in their own name with almost the same rights as citizens, and no permit, government approval or residency is needed to buy. The limits that matter are specific: no foreign ownership within 10 km of a land border, public beach strips and most island land that can only be held by concession, and the difference between titled land and mere possession rights. This guide covers those rules, the buying steps, closing costs, annual property tax and the residency routes tied to property, checked against current sources on October 17, 2026.

The Legal Framework

Global Property Guide sums up the position: foreigners have "almost the same opportunities as citizens" when buying property in Panama. Once a property is registered in the Public Registry (Registro Público) with its own finca (folio) number, it can be bought and sold by any person or company, whatever its nationality. Panama uses the US dollar as legal tender alongside the balboa, which is pegged at par, so there is no currency conversion on a dollar purchase.

The restrictions worth knowing:

  • 10 km border zone: Article 291 of the Constitution bars any foreign person, and any Panamanian company with foreign capital, from owning land less than 10 kilometres from a national border. In practice that means rural land near Costa Rica (Chiriquí, Bocas del Toro) and Colombia (Darién).
  • Beaches: Under Ley 80 of 2009, the beach strip (ribera de playa) measured inland from the high-tide line is public and cannot be owned: 22 metres on the Pacific and 10 metres on the Caribbean. The rest of the 200-metre coastal zone behind it can be titled to private owners, and public access to beaches must be kept open.
  • Islands: Island land is generally available only by government concession. Ley 80 (Arts. 13–14) allows titling only in limited cases, such as long-standing proven possession or areas the Cabinet declares special development zones.
  • Possession rights: Some rural and coastal land is held only as derecho posesorio (a right to occupy untitled land). It carries much more risk than titled land; have your attorney confirm exactly what is being sold.

The Buying Process, Step by Step

Global Property Guide puts the timeline at about 1–3 months from accepted offer to registered title.

1. Title search and due diligence

Hire a licensed Panamanian attorney before paying any deposit. Your attorney searches the Public Registry for the registered owner, mortgages, liens and other encumbrances, and checks that the annual property tax is paid. That last check matters: the tax is charged on the property itself, whoever owns it, and an unpaid balance ranks ahead of other liens.

2. Promise agreement

Buyer and seller sign a promesa de compraventa that fixes the price, closing date and conditions. The deposit is negotiated. If it is agreed as arras, the Civil Code (Art. 1224) lets the buyer walk away by losing it, or the seller by returning it doubled. Otherwise the remedies are whatever the contract says, so have counsel review it before you sign.

3. Public deed and transfer tax

The transfer deed (escritura pública) is signed before a Panamanian notary. The 2% real estate transfer tax (ITBI) is charged on the higher of the price or the registered value. That registered value is the property's cadastral value plus improvements, increased by 5% of the cadastral value for each full calendar year the seller has owned it. By law the ITBI is the seller's tax, and it must be paid before the transfer, with proof of payment recorded in the deed. A private agreement cannot transfer the tax to the buyer, according to tax lawyer Osvaldo Lau in La Prensa, but sellers may build it into the price, so compare offers on an all-in basis.

4. Registration

The notary's deed is registered at the Public Registry. Registration is what makes the transfer effective against third parties. Do not treat the purchase as finished until your attorney confirms the new registration.

Put together, a buyer should budget roughly 2–3% on top of the price for attorney, notary and registry costs (about 2.4% using the figures above). The seller's 3% is an advance payment of income tax on the sale, not a separate capital gains tax. The seller can accept it as final or pay 10% on the actual gain and credit the 3% against it. It does not come out of your pocket, but it affects how sellers price.

New homes: the 2% transfer tax in 2026

For decades the first sale of a new home was exempt from the transfer tax. Ley 468 of April 24, 2025 repealed that exemption, Ley 472 postponed the repeal until the end of 2025, and from January 1, 2026 first sales paid the full 2%. Ley 546 of August 31, 2026 brought back partial relief. On the first sale of a new home priced up to US$200,000, the first US$120,000 of the taxable value is exempt and the excess is taxed at stepped rates from 0.5% to 1.8%, provided the sale closes within two years of the occupancy permit. Homes above US$200,000 get no relief.

Annual Property Tax: Low and Tiered

Panama's property tax (impuesto de inmuebles) is set by Ley 66 of 2017, in force since January 1, 2019, and applies equally to foreign and Panamanian owners. Rates are marginal: each one applies only to the slice of registered value in its band. They depend on whether you register the home as your primary residence (patrimonio familiar tributario / vivienda principal) by sworn declaration to the tax authority (DGI).

Example: a home with a registered value of US$180,000 pays 0.5% on US$60,000, or about US$300 a year, as a registered primary residence. As a rental it pays 0.6% on US$150,000, or about US$900. The tax is paid in three instalments, due by April 30, August 31 and December 31.

New construction: the long 20-, 10- and 5-year exemptions on improvements apply only to buildings whose construction permit was issued before January 1, 2019. For newer projects, a first home bought as your primary residence with a registered value of US$120,000–300,000 is exempt for 3 years. The 3 years run from the occupancy permit or the registry inscription, whichever comes first. Ask your attorney whether a unit carries an exemption and when it ends.

Can Buying Property Lead to Residency?

Yes. If you're comparing Panama's thresholds against Colombia's investor visa or Costa Rica's rentista programme, our free market quiz maps these pathways side by side. The two main property-linked routes:

Friendly Nations visa

Citizens of about 50 countries on Panama's Friendly Nations list can apply with titled real estate worth at least US$200,000; a US$200,000 three-year fixed deposit is the alternative. The list was most recently updated by Executive Decree 16 of August 31, 2026, which added Ecuador, and it includes the United States, Canada, Spain, the UK and many other European countries. Approval gives a 2-year provisional residency, after which you can apply for permanent residency. It does not by itself give the right to work: a separate work permit from the Ministry of Labour is needed.

Qualified Investor visa

Executive Decree No. 17 of September 8, 2026, in force since September 16, 2026, grants permanent residency directly. The real estate minimum is US$300,000 for first-sale (new) property and US$500,000 for resale property, and the investment must be held for at least 5 years. Naturalization is a separate, discretionary step. The Constitution (Art. 10) requires five years of continuous residence, Spanish, basic knowledge of Panamanian geography, history and political organization, and renouncing your other nationality.

Mortgage Financing for Foreigners

Many Panamanian banks lend to foreign buyers, but expect a larger down payment than residents and stricter documentation, including proof of the source of funds. Mortgages are in US dollars. The average non-preferential mortgage rate was about 6.2% in April 2026 (Superintendencia de Bancos data reported by nexo.la), and banks' posted rates ran up to about 8–9%. Many foreign buyers pay cash to keep closing simple.

Key Things to Verify Before Signing

  • Titled vs. concession vs. possession: confirm that a beach, island or rural property has a registered title, not just a concession or derecho posesorio.
  • Border distance: rural land must be more than 10 km from a border.
  • Transfer tax: confirm the seller has paid the ITBI before the deed is signed. On a new home, ask whether the Ley 546 relief applies.
  • Property tax status: check that the tax is paid up to date, and whether a new-build exemption is active and when it ends.
  • Condo (PH) fees: monthly building fees in Panama City commonly run about US$1.25–2.50 per m² (roughly US$140–300 for a typical apartment), and more in luxury towers. Build them into your net yield.
  • Safety: the US State Department rates Panama Level 2 ("Exercise Increased Caution") and marks parts of Darién and the Mosquito Gulf as "Do Not Travel"; check before buying rural land there.

The Bottom Line

Panama is one of the most straightforward property markets in Latin America for foreign buyers. You can own titled property outright in your own name from day one, in US dollars, through a public registry. The rules that matter are practical. Avoid border land, check whether beach and island land is titled, confirm the seller has paid the 2% transfer tax, and confirm whether a property is first-sale or resale if the Qualified Investor visa is your goal (US$300,000 vs US$500,000). FAQ: Buying Property in Panama as a Foreigner

Can a foreigner own property in Panama?

Yes. Foreigners can own titled property in their own name, or through a company or foundation, with almost the same rights as citizens and no residency requirement. The Constitution (Art. 291) bars foreign ownership of land within 10 km of a border, beach strips are public, and most island land is held only by concession.

Who pays the 2% transfer tax in Panama?

By law the 2% transfer tax (ITBI) is the seller's tax, charged on the higher of the price or the registered value, and it must be paid before the transfer. A private agreement cannot transfer that tax to the buyer, though sellers may build it into the price. Since Ley 546 of August 2026, the first sale of a new home priced up to US$200,000 is exempt on its first US$120,000.

How much property tax do foreigners pay in Panama?

The same as citizens. A registered primary residence pays nothing on the first US$120,000 of registered value, then 0.5% up to US$700,000 and 0.7% above. Other property pays 0% up to US$30,000, then 0.6%, 0.8% and 1% in bands. The tax is due in three instalments: April 30, August 31 and December 31.
