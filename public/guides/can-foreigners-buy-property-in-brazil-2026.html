Can Foreigners Buy Property in Brazil? A Complete Legal Guide

By Charles Brossy May 12, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026 9 min read

The short answer: yes — for urban property, Brazil is one of the most straightforward foreign-ownership markets in Latin America. Foreigners can hold full, direct title to apartments, houses, and commercial units in São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, Florianópolis, or Salvador without special government permits, foreign-ownership quotas, or mandatory trusts. The critical distinction is between urban and rural property: rural land is tightly regulated under a 1971 law that caps how much a foreigner can own and prohibits purchases near Brazil's borders. For the vast majority of foreign investors — buying a beach apartment or a city condo — none of those restrictions apply.

The Legal Foundation

Brazil's Federal Constitution guarantees foreigners resident in Brazil the same rights as Brazilian nationals, and the Civil Code extends full real property ownership rights to non-residents purchasing urban property. There is no equivalent of Mexico's coastal Restricted Zone, no mandatory escrow or bank trust, and no cap on the number of urban properties a foreigner may own. Unlike some of its neighbours, Brazil does not require government approval to complete an urban residential purchase. The complexity that does exist is administrative — and it is manageable with the right local support.

Step 1 — Get a CPF (Brazilian Tax ID)

Before any property transaction can be signed in Brazil, a foreign buyer must obtain a CPF (Cadastro de Pessoa Física) — the Brazilian Individual Taxpayer Registry issued by the Receita Federal (Federal Revenue Service). Without a CPF you cannot sign a deed, open a bank account, or register an investment with the central bank. The good news: non-residents can apply for a CPF in person at a Brazilian consulate in their home country at no charge (see our tip on getting a CPF from abroad, including the yearly update foreigners abroad must now make). Your Brazilian attorney can handle the paperwork, but it is administratively simple. Planning to live there? See the Brazil retirement visa income requirement.

Step 2 — Register Your Foreign Capital

When you wire purchase funds from abroad, that capital transfer must be registered with the Banco Central do Brasil through the ROF/RDE-IED electronic system. This registration is what allows you to legally repatriate sale proceeds and rental income when you exit the investment. It is also the legal prerequisite for any investor residency application. Your Brazilian bank or attorney processes the registration at the time of the wire transfer. As with Colombia's Formulario 4, trying to backfill this later creates serious compliance headaches — register each transfer when it happens.

The Buying Process, Step by Step

Due Diligence and the Purchase Agreement

Engage a Brazilian property attorney (separate from the real estate agent) to pull the property's matrícula from the local Cartório de Registro de Imóveis — Brazil's public property registry. The matrícula shows the full chain of title, any mortgages or liens, and any encumbrances. Your attorney will also verify there are no outstanding IPTU (annual property tax) debts or condominium fee arrears, both of which can attach to the property. Budget $1,500–$3,000 USD for a competent bilingual attorney, plus sworn translation fees for any foreign-language documents. A binding Contrato de Compra e Venda (purchase and sale agreement) is then signed, with a deposit of typically 10–20% of the purchase price.

Signing the Escritura Pública

The final deed — the escritura pública de compra e venda — is executed before a Brazilian notary public (Tabelião). Both buyer and seller, or their duly appointed attorneys-in-fact, must appear. The notary calculates applicable taxes and fees at closing. A cash purchase from signed agreement to registered deed typically completes in 30–60 days.

Registration

Legal ownership transfers only upon registration of the deed at the local Cartório de Registro de Imóveis. Your notary submits the deed; turnaround is typically 5–20 business days. Until the new matrícula shows your name, the transaction is not legally complete.

Closing Costs: What to Budget

Brazil's closing costs are higher than most of its neighbours. Plan for total buyer-side closing costs of 5–8% of the purchase price. The main items:

  • ITBI (transfer tax): Set by each municipality, typically 2–4%. São Paulo charges 3%, paid by the buyer (see our São Paulo ITBI tip and the closing-costs table for 11 countries). Check the rate with the city where you buy.
  • Cartório fees (notary + registry): Combined 1–2% of the property value, covering the deed drafting and title registration.
  • Attorney fees: $1,500–$3,000 USD for a standard residential purchase.
  • Sworn translations: Required for any foreign-language documents; a few hundred to a few thousand BRL.
  • Tax advisory: Strongly recommended for non-residents; roughly $500–$1,500 USD.

Sellers pay a capital gains tax (Imposto de Renda sobre Ganho de Capital) on any profit, at progressive federal rates of 15–22.5% depending on the gain amount. As a buyer, this is not your liability — but it shapes seller behaviour and can affect negotiating leverage.

Annual Property Tax: Modest but Municipally Variable

Brazil's annual IPTU (Imposto Predial e Territorial Urbano) is levied by each municipality on the official assessed value (valor venal), which typically sits below market value. Effective rates for residential properties generally fall between 0.3% and 1% of the assessed value per year. A $250,000 USD apartment in São Paulo might generate an annual IPTU bill of $500–$1,200 USD. Most municipalities offer discounts of 5–15% for paying the full year upfront in January.

Rural Land: Different Rules Apply

Law No. 5.709/71 restricts foreign ownership of rural land. A foreign individual may own at most 50 módulos de exploração indefinida (a unit of area that varies by region); up to 3 módulos can be bought freely, while larger purchases fall under INCRA authorisation rules, and foreign companies may buy rural land only for approved agricultural, livestock, industrial or settlement projects. Inside the 150 km border strip (Faixa de Fronteira), Law 6.634/79 bars any rural transaction that gives a foreigner ownership, possession or another real right without the prior assent of the national security council (today the Conselho de Defesa Nacional), which covers substantial territory in the Amazon, Pantanal and frontier states. If you are considering farmland, timberland or agricultural investment, hire counsel who specialise in these laws. For urban residential and commercial property, none of this applies.

Can Buying Property Lead to Residency?

Yes — through Brazil's VIPER programme (Visto de Investidor — Pessoa Estrangeira Residente). A minimum real estate investment of BRL 1,000,000 (about US$186,000 at the Banco Central PTAX rate of R$5.37 on October 29, 2026) in urban property qualifies a foreign buyer for a four-year residency permit that can later be made indefinite. If the property is located in Brazil's North or Northeast regions, the threshold drops to BRL 700,000 (about US$130,000) — a significant incentive for buyers eyeing coastal Nordeste markets like Fortaleza, Natal, or Recife. The first permit runs for four years; if the investment is still held, it can then be changed to residence of indefinite duration, provided the holder has spent at least 14 days in Brazil every two years (Normative Resolution 36/2018, Arts. 3–6). Brazil's Market in Late 2026: The Numbers

Prices are rising faster than inflation. The FipeZAP index, which tracks asking prices in 56 Brazilian cities, rose 0.58% in September 2026 and 5.51% over 12 months, ahead of consumer inflation (4.45%). The average asking price was R$10,015 per m². Among the state capitals, Vitória led at R$15,905/m², followed by Florianópolis (R$13,607/m²), São Paulo (R$12,206/m²), Curitiba (R$11,766/m²) and Rio de Janeiro (R$11,302/m²). São Paulo was one of the slowest-rising big cities, up 3.64% in a year, while Salvador (+10.38%) and Fortaleza (+10.20%) were the fastest risers in the Northeast.

Money is getting slightly cheaper. The Banco Central's Copom set the Selic policy rate at 13.75% from September 17, 2026, down from 14.50% in early June. Brazilian loan rates stay high by US standards, which is why most foreign buyers pay cash. The real traded at about R$5.37 per US dollar on October 29, 2026 (PTAX), so a R$1 million São Paulo apartment costs roughly US$186,000. Currency moves can add to or wipe out a gain in reais when you convert back. For city detail, see our São Paulo city guide.

The Bottom Line

For urban property — which covers the apartments, condos, and beachfront units most foreign buyers are actually targeting — Brazil is a clean, open market. You own the title outright, in your own name, from the date of registration. The two things that catch foreign buyers off guard are the ITBI closing costs (higher than much of the region) and the capital registration requirement for repatriating funds. Get both right from the start, work with a bilingual Brazilian attorney, and the process is straightforward. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreigner own an apartment in Brazil without living there?

Yes. Non-residents can hold full, direct title to urban property such as apartments and houses in their own name, with no special permit, ownership quota or mandatory trust. You will need a CPF (Brazilian tax ID) before you can sign the deed.

What closing costs does a buyer pay in Brazil?

Plan for roughly 5–8% of the purchase price. The largest item is the municipal ITBI transfer tax, typically 2–4% (São Paulo charges 3%), plus about 1–2% in notary and registry fees and attorney fees.

Does buying property in Brazil give you residency?

It can, through the VIPER investor programme: an urban real estate investment of at least BRL 1,000,000, or BRL 700,000 in the North and Northeast regions, qualifies for a four-year residency permit that can later be made indefinite. The purchase capital must be registered with the Banco Central do Brasil.
