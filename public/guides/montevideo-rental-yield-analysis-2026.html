Montevideo Rental Yield Analysis 2026: What Investors Are Actually Earning

By Charles Brossy April 24, 2026 · Updated October 20, 2026 9 min read

Montevideo doesn't make the headlines the way Medellín or Buenos Aires do — and that's quietly working in investors' favour. As of October 2026, asking-price data put estimated gross rental yields at about 5–6% in the central and coastal barrios and 9–11% in some outer barrios, and Uruguay's legal framework lets foreigners buy on the same terms as citizens, with no residency required.

Why Montevideo Now

Uruguay has long been called the "Switzerland of South America" — stable institutions, rule of law, low corruption. The 2025–2029 National Budget Law (Law 20.446, promulgated December 16, 2025) created Uruguay Innova, a Presidency programme to coordinate the country's innovation ecosystem and improve productivity. It is a coordination programme, not a relocation scheme, so don't count on it to bring in tenants by itself.

The tenant demand shows up in the official rent data. INE's rental figures, based on lease contracts registered with the DGI, show the average rent in Pocitos up 7.9% year on year in pesos as of August 2026, and Cordón up 6.3%. Cordón also dominates new-build supply: it accounts for 26.4% of all Vivienda Promovida sales declared in Montevideo, according to the ANV's May 2026 report.

Rental Yields by Neighborhood

The yields below come from the Índice INGAR (September 2026), a monthly index published by a Montevideo brokerage. It sets average rents against asking prices, before vacancy, management, maintenance and taxes. Closing prices in Uruguay are usually below asking, so real yields can be a little higher. The rents quoted are INE's August 2026 averages across all registered contracts in each barrio, not rents for a specific unit size.

Pocitos & Punta Carretas — about 5%–5.3% gross

These are Montevideo's premium residential neighborhoods — tree-lined streets, beach access along the Rambla, dense café culture, and proximity to the Montevideo Shopping and World Trade Center office towers. Median asking prices for apartments were $3,956/sqm in Pocitos and $4,184/sqm in Punta Carretas as of September 2026. INE's average rent in August 2026 was UYU 28,381 (about $705) a month in Pocitos and UYU 29,670 (about $737) in Punta Carretas. INGAR's estimated gross yields are 5.3% and 5.1%.

Yields compress here because prices are high. These neighborhoods suit investors who want quality tenants and long-term price growth, not the highest current cash yield.

Cordón & Centro — about 6% gross

Cordón is the sweet spot for yield-focused investors. The median asking price was $3,228/sqm as of September 2026, and INE's average rent was UYU 24,730 (about $614) a month in August 2026, giving an estimated gross yield of 6.2%. The neighborhood is walkable, close to the university and has strong café and restaurant density. It is also where most new Vivienda Promovida units are being built.

Centro proper had a median asking price of $3,026/sqm (September 2026) and an average rent of UYU 23,125 (about $574) a month (August 2026), also about 6.2% gross. Quality varies a lot, and the middle half of Centro listings range from about $2,300 to $3,600/sqm. Some Centro blocks are office-heavy or poorly maintained. Target recent Vivienda Promovida buildings for newer stock and better tenants.

Carrasco — about 5.5% gross

Montevideo's wealthiest suburb had a median apartment asking price of $4,370/sqm as of September 2026, among the highest in the city. INGAR estimates the gross yield at 5.5%. INE's average Carrasco rent of UYU 34,787 (about $864) in August 2026 was 37% lower than a year earlier, but the August 2025 figure was based on only 10 contracts. Carrasco suits buyers purchasing for personal use or long-term family rental rather than pure yield.

Outer Barrios (Colón, Cerrito, Malvín Norte, La Teja) — about 9%–11% gross

On paper, the highest apartment yields in the September 2026 Índice INGAR are in Colón (11.1%, at a median asking price of $1,060/sqm), Cerrito (10.3%), Malvín Norte (9.7%) and La Teja (9.3%). In practice, these areas carry much higher operating risk: tenant arrears, harder property management and lower liquidity at resale. Investors unfamiliar with Montevideo should treat these figures with caution. A local property manager with experience in these barrios is non-negotiable if you go this route.

The Vivienda Promovida Advantage

Ley de Vivienda Promovida (Law 18.795) is the most investor-friendly mechanism in the Uruguayan residential market. Developers who build qualifying housing in designated zones receive tax breaks. According to the ANV's May 2026 price report, Vivienda Promovida units in Montevideo sold for an average of $2,411 per built sqm in the year to April 2026, and two-bedroom units averaged $166,175. Buyers of qualifying units get:

  • Exemption from property transfer tax (ITP) and VAT (IVA) on the first sale, provided it happens within ten years of the building's completion
  • Income tax exemption on rental income (IRPF for resident individuals, IRNR for non-residents, IRAE for companies) for the year the building is completed and the nine following years: 100% of the rent in zones designated by the housing ministry or when the lease uses the state rent-guarantee fund (FGA) or another approved guarantee, and 60% in all other cases. Leases must run at least 12 months.
  • Wealth tax (Impuesto al Patrimonio) exemption for each year the unit was rented for at least six months

The practical effect is that net yields on units with the full exemption are only marginally below gross yields during the exemption period. Unprotected rental income from conventional properties is taxed at 12%: IRPF for residents or IRNR for non-residents, as of October 2026.

Foreign buyers are fully eligible for Vivienda Promovida units with no restrictions.

Purchase Costs and Entry Friction

Uruguay's closing costs are transparent, though not trivial:

  • Transfer tax (ITP): 2% of the cadastral value, paid by the buyer (the seller pays another 2%). It is waived on the first sale of a Vivienda Promovida unit.
  • Notary fees: about 3% of the price plus 22% VAT, paid by the buyer
  • Agent commission: about 3% of the price plus 22% VAT, paid by each side
  • Registration and title certificates: extra, handled through the notary

Because ITP is charged on the cadastral value, which is usually below the market price, buyers using an agent should budget roughly 8–9% of the price on a conventional purchase. On a first-sale Vivienda Promovida unit the transfer tax falls away.

Annual property tax in Montevideo (Contribución Inmobiliaria) is charged by the Intendencia at progressive rates on the property's taxable value, not its market price. Check the exact bill for a given padrón with the Intendencia before you buy.

Foreigners can buy in personal name or through a Uruguayan corporation (SA or SRL). Corporate ownership offers liability protection and can simplify cross-border estate planning. Foreign buyers do not need residency to purchase.

The Residency Upside

Uruguay's tax-holiday regime for new residents was rewritten by the 2025–2029 National Budget Law (Law 20.446), in force from January 1, 2026. As of October 2026, someone who becomes a Uruguayan tax resident can opt to pay IRNR instead of IRPF on foreign-source capital income (such as dividends and interest from non-resident entities, and gains on those assets) for the year they become resident plus the ten following years. That income is effectively untaxed in Uruguay during the holiday. To qualify, a new resident must be physically present in Uruguay for more than 183 days in each year, or invest more than UI 12.5 million in real estate (roughly US$2 million), or put at least UI 625,000 a year into qualifying investment funds. They must also not have been a tax resident in the previous two years. When the holiday ends, residents can choose to pay IRPF at half the normal rate for five more years (subject to investment conditions) or pay a fixed annual amount. People who were already on the old regime keep it for the rest of their original term.

Legal residency is a separate process. It requires proof of a regular monthly income sufficient to live on, with no minimum property investment, so it is accessible to a wide range of investors and retirees.

Realistic Net Yield Estimate

Take a Vivienda Promovida two-bedroom in Cordón bought at $166,000, close to the ANV's Montevideo average for two-bedroom units. Assume it rents for $860/month, which matches INGAR's 6.2% gross-yield estimate for Cordón:

  • Gross annual rent: $10,320
  • Gross yield: 6.2%
  • Less management fee (assumed 8%): -$826
  • Less maintenance/reserve (assumed 1% of price): -$1,660
  • Less income tax (100% Vivienda Promovida exemption): $0
  • Net yield: approximately 4.7%

That assumes no vacancy. At 95% occupancy (one vacant month every 20), net yield falls to about 4.4%. If the unit only qualifies for the 60% exemption, the remaining 40% of rent is taxable at 12%, which costs up to about $495 a year here and also brings net yield to about 4.4%. For a USD-priced asset in a country with strong rule of law, that compares well with other markets in the region.

Key Risks to Underwrite

No market is without risk. Montevideo's main ones:

  • Peso depreciation: Rents are typically denominated in pesos, not USD. The UYU has historically devalued against the dollar, which shrinks USD-equivalent yields over time. Note that the ANV's May 2026 report shows Vivienda Promovida prices in Montevideo up in dollars over the past year but down in inflation-indexed units (UI). Negotiate USD leases where possible, particularly with expat tenants.
  • Vivienda Promovida supply: The incentive program keeps adding new supply, particularly in Cordón, which could cap rent growth in the neighborhoods it targets.
  • Liquidity: Montevideo is not a deep market. Resale can take months even for correctly priced properties, and overpriced listings sit longer. Plan your exit horizon accordingly.

The Bottom Line

Montevideo offers one of the most credible risk-adjusted rental yield propositions in Latin America. Its strengths are steady rent growth in the core barrios, a meaningful tax exemption through the Vivienda Promovida framework, and one of the region's most investor-friendly legal environments. Cordón and Centro are the value plays for yield; Pocitos and Punta Carretas for capital preservation and tenant quality. Either way, closing costs are transparent and the legal process is straightforward for foreign buyers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What rental yield can I expect in Montevideo?

As of October 2026, INGAR's asking-price index puts gross yields at about 5–5.3% in Pocitos and Punta Carretas, about 6% in Cordón and Centro, about 5.5% in Carrasco, and 9–11% in some outer barrios such as Colón (11.1%) and Cerrito (10.3%). Net yields are lower once tax, management, maintenance and vacancy are counted.

How is rental income taxed in Uruguay?

Rental income is taxed at 12%: under IRPF for tax residents and under IRNR for non-residents. Vivienda Promovida units can be exempt from IRPF on rent for the year of completion plus nine years — 100% in designated zones or with an approved rent guarantee, 60% otherwise — for leases of at least 12 months.

What does it cost to buy property in Montevideo?

Buyers typically pay a notary fee of about 3% plus VAT, an agent commission of about 3% plus VAT, and the 2% property transfer tax (ITP) on cadastral value, with the seller paying another 2%. Budget roughly 8–9% with an agent; ITP is waived on the first sale of a Vivienda Promovida unit.
