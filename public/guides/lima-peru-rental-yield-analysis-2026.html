Lima, Peru: Rental Yield Analysis by District (2026)

By Charles Brossy May 8, 2026 9 min read

Lima is one of Latin America's most underrated investment markets. The city of 11 million has a functioning property registry, full foreign ownership rights, and rental yields that average 6.3% gross citywide — well ahead of comparable districts in Santiago or Mexico City. But the numbers vary sharply by neighborhood, and the districts with the highest rents are not the ones with the best returns.

The Market Snapshot: What Prices Look Like in 2026

The median price per square meter across Lima sits at approximately $1,900/sqm (S/ 6,384) as of early 2026, with a median apartment price near $152,000. That citywide average, however, obscures a massive spread: the upscale coastal districts of Miraflores, San Isidro, and Barranco command $1,700–$2,400/sqm, while peripheral districts fall below $900/sqm.

For foreign investors, the relevant range is the $1,700–$2,400 band — this is where professionally managed rentals, expat tenants, and consistent demand intersect. The question is which end of that premium spectrum delivers the better yield.

District-by-District Yield Breakdown

Surquillo: Best Yield per Dollar Invested

Surquillo is Lima's most compelling yield play right now. Bordering Miraflores on three sides, it offers Miraflores-adjacent walkability at a meaningfully lower purchase price. Estimated net yields run 5.2–5.3% across apartment types — the strongest net figure of any sought-after Lima district. Developers including Besco and Senda have launched several mid-rise projects along Surquillo's Miraflores-border blocks over the past 18 months, and price appreciation of 8–15% over the past two to three years has not yet eliminated the yield advantage. This is where the smart money is concentrating in 2026.

Barranco: Lifestyle Premium, Solid Returns

Barranco — Lima's bohemian arts district — sits south of Miraflores along the Pacific cliffs. Prices have appreciated sharply as gentrification reached the inland streets behind the tourist core, yet gross yields still run 6–7% for well-located units. Demand comes from foreign residents, digital nomads, and affluent Limeños priced out of Miraflores. Short-term rental performance (Airbnb) is particularly strong in Barranco given its cultural cachet. The risk is that appreciation has already been priced in on the best streets, so underwriting conservatively matters here.

Jesús María and Magdalena del Mar: Mid-Tier Efficiency

These two districts sit in Lima's "second ring" — less fashionable than Miraflores or Barranco, but with solid residential demand from professionals and families. Purchase prices are 25–35% lower than prime districts, and gross yields typically land in the 6.5–7.5% range. Jesús María in particular has seen a surge of new-build launches as developers seek land at viable costs. For yield-focused investors willing to forgo the prestige address, these districts offer the strongest cash-on-cash returns in metropolitan Lima. Miraflores: Trophy Asset, Compressed Yield

Miraflores is Lima's most desirable address — the Parque Kennedy, the Larcomar mall perched on the Pacific cliffs, the walkable restaurant strip. But desirability has pushed prices high enough to compress returns. Net yields average just 4.1% in Miraflores, with high-end units near the malecón sometimes dipping to 3.5–4.0%. Properties sell within 16–21 days and command a pricing premium that buyers are willing to pay — but investors should not expect Miraflores to work as an income property unless they have strong appreciation assumptions built in. It remains an excellent long-term hold; it is not a yield story.

San Isidro: Corporate and Diplomatic Demand

San Isidro is Lima's financial district — home to corporate offices, embassies, and international hotel brands. Rental demand from corporate tenants and expat professionals keeps occupancy high, and gross yields run 6–7% on well-positioned units. Prices above S/ 9,000/sqm (roughly $2,400–$2,700 at current rates) are common for newer buildings near the financial core. San Isidro suits investors who want institutional-quality tenants and low vacancy over raw yield maximization.

What Foreigners Need to Know Before Buying

Peru grants foreign nationals the same property ownership rights as Peruvian citizens. You can purchase in your personal name — no local partner, no trust structure, no residency prerequisite. The only restriction: properties within 50 kilometers of an international border require special authorization, which does not affect Lima at all given the city's location.

Closing costs for resale properties run 4.5–6.5% of the purchase price. The main tax component is the alcabala (transfer tax), with the first S/ 55,000 (~$16,400) of any transaction exempt. New-build purchases from developers typically carry lower closing costs of 2–4%, making off-plan a cost-efficient entry point. Annual property tax (predial) uses progressive brackets of 0.2%, 0.6%, and 1.0% of assessed value — a mid-range apartment generating roughly S/ 2,000–6,000 (~$600–$1,800) per year.

Mortgage financing is available to foreign buyers but more restrictive than for residents: expect 60–70% loan-to-value ratios, interest rates of 8–12% in soles, and loan terms of 10–20 years. Most foreign investors purchase with cash or developer stage payments to avoid the financing hurdle and currency exposure. The Investment Case in 2026

Lima's appeal as an investment market rests on three structural supports. First, housing supply has chronically lagged demand — Lima adds roughly 60,000 new households per year but historically permits far fewer units, particularly in the mid-to-upper segments. Second, dollarized leases are standard in prime districts, eliminating currency risk for foreign investors earning rents in USD. Third, Peru's economy has outperformed regional peers in GDP growth for most of the past decade, underpinning professional income growth and rental demand.

The risks are real too: political uncertainty has been a recurring theme in Peru, and Lima's property market is illiquid by developed-market standards — expect 30–90 days to find a qualified buyer even in prime districts. Due diligence through a licensed Peruvian attorney is essential: verify registry title, confirm no liens or judicial annotations, and check that any existing structure has valid municipal permits (licencia de obra). Q1 2026 Update: Lince Emerges as a Yield Leader

Transaction data through Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 flags a district not yet on most foreign investors' radar: Lince, sandwiched between Miraflores and San Isidro. Lince is now posting gross yields of approximately 7.1% — the highest of any tracked Lima district — driven by a large stock of older apartment buildings trading at lower price-per-sqm multiples while rents have kept pace with the upscale corridors on either side. Magdalena del Mar and San Miguel follow closely at roughly 6.7% each, reinforcing the mid-tier efficiency story outlined above.

Market volume held firm: Lima closed approximately 25,000 residential units sold in 2025, in line with 2024 and confirming that overall transaction activity has not slowed despite affordability pressures. Annual price growth in premium districts averaged 10%, compressing entry-level yields modestly but still leaving Lima's mid-tier well ahead of regional peers such as Santiago or São Paulo at equivalent price points.

The Bottom Line

Lima offers a rare combination in 2026: a major capital city with full foreign ownership rights, USD-denominated rents in prime districts, and gross yields averaging 6.3% citywide. Surquillo and Jesús María are the yield leaders; Barranco and San Isidro balance income with capital appreciation; Miraflores is a long-term hold rather than an income play. The key is buying in the right sub-market for your objectives — and conducting thorough title due diligence before committing capital.
